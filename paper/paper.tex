\begin{abstract}
  Self-play frameworks for multi-hop question answering aim to build automated curricula by jointly training a proposer, which generates challenging questions, and a solver, which answers them. However, existing approaches largely overlook a fundamental bottleneck: the quality of the reward signal provided to the proposer. When the proposer is rewarded only according to whether the solver can answer a question, it may learn to generate questions that are superficially difficult but logically incomplete, thereby limiting the solver’s improvement.
In this paper, we propose a backward verifier to improve proposer training. Given a candidate answer, the backward verifier is trained to trace the hop chain in reverse through retrieval-augmented reasoning and determine whether the original question can be logically reconstructed from the answer and supporting evidence. This provides an explicit consistency signal that penalizes questions whose reasoning chains cannot be recovered from their answers. By augmenting the proposer’s objective with this backward verification reward, our framework encourages the generation of questions that are not only difficult but also logically coherent and pedagogically useful. Experiments on multi-hop QA benchmarks show that our approach consistently trains stronger solvers than self-play baselines, improving exact match by [X]\%. Further analysis demonstrates that the proposed verifier leads to questions with more coherent hop structure and better evidence alignment, highlighting the importance of reward quality in self-play curriculum construction.

% 다중 홉 QA에서도 이러한 자체 플레이 방식이 적용되고 있다. 기존 연구들은 제안자가 문서에서 출발하여 여러 사실적 홉을 연결함으로써 다중 홉 질문을 생성하고, 해결자가 검색 기반 추론을 통해 해당 홉 체인을 복원하며 답을 찾도록 학습시킨다. 이 접근은 사람이 직접 다중 홉 질문을 작성해야 하는 비용을 줄이고, 학습 과정에서 보다 동적인 질문 분포를 만들 수 있다는 점에서 유망하다.

Recent progress in self-improving language models \cite{ahmadian2024back, Spiral, Evolvesearch, zhao2026absolute} suggests that models can improve not only by imitating human-labeled data, but also by generating and learning from their own training signals. Across domains such as mathematical reasoning \cite{huang2026rzero}, code generation, and question answering, reinforcement learning from self-generated rollouts has enabled models to explore solution strategies beyond those available through supervised imitation alone. For question answering, this raises a natural question: can a model construct its own curriculum of increasingly difficult questions?
This question is particularly important for multi-hop question answering (QA). Unlike single-hop QA, multi-hop QA requires a model to connect pieces of evidence distributed across multiple documents and derive an answer through a coherent reasoning chain. A useful training question, therefore, should not merely contain an answer. It should require the solver to recover a valid chain of evidence, while ensuring that the chain is actually grounded in the underlying corpus.
However, most existing QA training methods rely on fixed datasets constructed before training begins. The questions, answers, and supervision signals are predetermined, and the difficulty distribution remains static even as the model improves. As a result, the model cannot actively seek out new questions that expose its current weaknesses, and the curriculum cannot adapt to the solver’s evolving capabilities. This limitation is especially pronounced in multi-hop QA, where progress depends on training examples that are not only difficult, but also grounded in coherent evidence chains.

Self-play offers a natural alternative. In a self-play framework, a proposer generates questions and a solver learns to answer them through repeated interaction. Ideally, the proposer generates questions near the frontier of the solver’s current capability, and the solver improves by learning to answer them. This process forms an automated curriculum without requiring human-authored questions, allowing the training distribution to evolve as the solver improves.
Recent work has instantiated this idea for multi-hop QA. The proposer constructs multi-hop questions by chaining factual hops from source documents, and the solver answers them by issuing search queries to recover the underlying evidence chain. This approach reduces dependence on manually written multi-hop questions and enables a more dynamic question distribution. However, it also introduces a critical bottleneck: the quality of self-play training is bounded by the quality of the proposer.
Existing self-play methods often overlook the quality of the reward signal provided to the proposer. Many approaches reward generated questions primarily according to whether the solver answers them correctly. Yet solver correctness alone is an insufficient measure of question quality. A question that the solver fails to answer may be difficult because it requires genuine multi-hop reasoning, but it may also be difficult because it is logically incomplete, underspecified, weakly grounded, or effectively unanswerable.
These two forms of difficulty have very different consequences for learning. The former provides useful pressure on the solver and exposes its limitations. The latter provides little or no meaningful supervision, because no coherent reasoning path can reliably lead to the answer. Nevertheless, an answer-based reward does not distinguish between useful difficulty and spurious difficulty. As a result, the proposer may learn to generate questions that are superficially hard but logically defective, thereby degrading the curriculum and limiting solver improvement.

Our central claim is that self-play for multi-hop QA should optimize not merely for difficult question generation, but for verifiably difficult question generation. A good multi-hop question should challenge the solver, but its answer should also be recoverable through a coherent evidence chain. In other words, difficulty should arise from the need to connect multiple facts, not from ambiguity, missing evidence, or broken reasoning structure.
To this end, we propose a backward verifier for improving proposer training. Given a generated question and a candidate answer, the backward verifier performs retrieval-augmented reasoning in reverse: starting from the candidate answer, it traces the required hop chain backward toward the entities and constraints specified in the question. Rather than merely checking whether the candidate answer matches a gold string, the verifier evaluates whether the answer is logically supported by a recoverable multi-hop reasoning path.
This backward verification signal provides richer supervision for the proposer. Questions whose reasoning chains cannot be reconstructed receive low reward, while questions with coherent and evidence-grounded hop structures receive high reward. By augmenting answer-based rewards with backward verification, the proposer is encouraged to generate questions that are not only difficult for the solver, but also answerable, logically consistent, and useful for training.

We integrate the backward verifier into an iterative self-play pipeline. At each iteration, the proposer generates multi-hop questions, the solver answers them through retrieval-augmented reasoning, and the backward verifier evaluates whether the solver’s candidate answers can be traced back to the question constraints through valid evidence chains. The resulting verification signal is used to refine proposer training, improving the quality of questions generated in subsequent iterations. Through this feedback loop, the self-play curriculum becomes increasingly composed of questions that are challenging yet logically verifiable.
We evaluate our method on both single-hop and multi-hop QA benchmarks. Experiments show that improving proposer rewards through backward verification leads to stronger solvers than existing self-play baselines. Further analyses of generated questions demonstrate that our method improves evidence alignment, hop-chain coherence, and answerability. These results indicate that the key to effective self-play in multi-hop QA is not only training the solver, but also improving the quality of the question distribution on which the solver learns.


\section{Related Work}

Multi-hop question answering requires a model to retrieve and chain multiple pieces of evidence before arriving at a final answer.
Early work addressed this through supervised decomposition --- breaking a question into sub-questions and solving each independently --- or through iterative retrieval pipelines that alternately retrieve and reason over passages.
More recent methods train models to actively issue search queries within a reasoning chain, interleaving retrieval and generation across multiple turns.
Representative approaches such as IRCoT structure reasoning as an interleaved chain-of-thought and retrieval process, while search-augmented RL methods (e.g., Search-R1, R-Zero) train models end-to-end to decide when and what to search.
Despite strong empirical results, these methods operate over a fixed training distribution: the set of questions and their difficulty are determined once, before training begins, and do not adapt as the model improves.

Self-evolution refers to a family of methods in which a model generates its own training data, receives outcome-based feedback, and iteratively improves without human annotation.
In mathematical reasoning and code generation, this has produced remarkable results: models trained purely on self-generated rollouts, rewarded by a verifier, surpass those trained on large supervised datasets.
The self-play variant extends this idea to two-model systems --- a generator and a discriminator, or a proposer and a solver --- where both models co-evolve.
Applied to QA, self-play allows the proposer to continuously adapt question difficulty to the solver's current capability, creating a natural curriculum.
Recent work such as DrZero instantiates this loop for multi-hop QA: the proposer chains factual hops starting from a Wikipedia passage, and the solver trains to recover the answer via search.
A shared limitation across these works is that the proposer is trained with only a coarse difficulty signal (e.g., solver accuracy), and its quality is not explicitly optimized beyond that.

Curriculum learning --- presenting training examples in order of increasing difficulty --- has long been known to accelerate learning and improve generalization.
In the self-play setting, the ideal curriculum is one where question difficulty co-evolves with solver capability: questions that are too easy provide no learning signal, while questions that are too hard yield no reward.
Existing self-play methods approximate this by rewarding the proposer when a fixed fraction of solver attempts succeed.
However, this signal is noisy: a question may appear difficult for the current solver simply because it is malformed or unanswerable, not because it lies at the true capability frontier.
More precise difficulty estimation --- for instance, by tracking how solver performance on the same questions changes across iterations --- provides a cleaner curriculum signal.

Bidirectional reasoning uses the structure of a problem in both forward and backward directions to improve inference quality.
In multi-hop QA, forward reasoning follows the hop chain from the question's starting entity to the answer; backward reasoning starts from a candidate answer and traces hops in reverse to verify consistency with the question.
This idea connects to self-consistency methods and chain-of-thought verification, where a model checks its own answers by re-deriving them from a different starting point.
Recent work on bidirectional training (e.g., RevThink) shows that training on both directions simultaneously improves reasoning robustness.
We adapt this principle to the self-play setting: rather than training the proposer or solver bidirectionally, we train a dedicated verifier to perform backward hop search over solver predictions, providing a structured verification signal that is richer than binary exact-match feedback.

\section{Method}

We build on the multi-hop question answering self-play setting of DR-Zero.
The overall framework consists of three models: a proposer model $\pi_P$ that generates questions, a solver model $\pi_S$ that answers the generated questions, and a backward verifier $\pi_V$ that evaluates the logical verifiability of question--answer pairs.
The proposer generates multi-hop questions from raw Wikipedia passages, while the solver answers them through retrieval-augmented reasoning.
The backward verifier assesses whether a solver's candidate answer is logically supported by the evidence chain underlying the question, and the resulting verification signal is used as a question-level reward for subsequent proposer training.

Importantly, the backward verification reward is not available at the moment of question generation.
It is computed post hoc: after the proposer generates a question, the solver attempts to answer it, and the verifier evaluates the solver's candidate answers.
Thus, we use the backward verification signal as a delayed question-level reward assigned to the proposer rollout that generated the question.
We do not modify the HRPO algorithm itself; instead, we reshape the proposer reward used by HRPO with the backward verification signal.

\subsection{Multi-Hop QA self-play setting}

We follow the multi-hop QA self-play environment of DR-Zero.
Given a raw Wikipedia passage $d$, the proposer model $\pi_P$ generates a multi-hop question $q$.
Specifically, the proposer selects a starting entity appearing in the passage and sequentially chains $n$ factual hops through search.
The generated question contains the starting entity, and the gold answer $a^*$ is the entity reached at the $n$-th hop.
Thus, the generated questions are not single-document factual questions, but multi-hop questions that require connecting entities and relations across multiple passages.
The solver model $\pi_S$ is trained to answer the generated question $q$.
Rather than producing an answer in a single step, the solver issues search queries over multiple turns and reasons over the retrieved passages.
Through this process, the solver attempts to recover the hop chain constructed by the proposer and produces a final candidate answer $\hat{a}$.
The training algorithm differs by model role.
The solver $\pi_S$ is trained with GRPO.
GRPO is a reinforcement learning algorithm that estimates relative advantages from grouped rollout samples for the same question, without requiring a separate value model.
In contrast, following DR-Zero, the proposer $\pi_P$ is trained with HRPO.
HRPO computes question-level rewards from the responses of a solver group and optimizes the proposer to generate questions that are useful for solver learning.

The overall training loop runs for $T$ iterations.
At each iteration $t$, the proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ generates a dataset of questions from Wikipedia passages.
The solver $\pi_S^{(t)}$ then performs retrieval-augmented reasoning on the generated questions and produces multiple answer rollouts for each question.
The solver is updated with GRPO based on the correctness of these rollouts.
Afterward, the proposer reward is computed post hoc from the solver outcomes and backward verification results, and this reward is used to update the proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ with HRPO.

\subsection{Backward Verification}

In self-play frameworks such as DR-Zero, the standard reward for the proposer is primarily based on question difficulty.
For example, if all solvers in a group answer a question correctly, the question is considered too easy and receives low reward.
Conversely, if no solver can answer the question, it is considered too hard and also receives low reward.
Questions with intermediate solver success rates are treated as lying near the solver's current capability frontier and receive higher reward.
This difficulty-based signal indicates how hard a question is for the solver, but not why it is hard.
This distinction is crucial in multi-hop QA.
A question may be difficult because it contains a well-formed multi-hop reasoning structure that genuinely challenges the solver.
However, a question may also be difficult because it is logically incomplete, internally inconsistent, missing necessary evidence, or effectively unanswerable.
A pure difficulty reward cannot distinguish between these cases, and may therefore encourage the proposer to generate questions that are superficially hard but not useful for training.

To address this ambiguity, we introduce a verifier model $\pi_V$ that performs backward hop search.
The goal of the backward verifier is to assess whether the solver's candidate answer can be logically supported by the reasoning chain required by the question.
Given a question $q$ and a solver-generated candidate answer $\hat{a}$, the verifier starts from $\hat{a}$, issues search queries, and traces the hop chain in reverse.
During this process, the verifier checks whether it can reach the starting entity, constraints, or intermediate evidence specified by the question.
If the reverse chain closes successfully, meaning that there exists a recoverable evidence path between the candidate answer and the question conditions, the verifier outputs \texttt{YES}.
Otherwise, if the retrieved evidence and reasoning process fail to connect the candidate answer to the question conditions, the verifier outputs \texttt{NO}.
Thus, the backward verifier does not merely check whether the candidate answer matches a target string; it evaluates whether the answer is supported by a recoverable multi-hop reasoning path required by the question.

\subsection{Training the Backward Verifier}

The backward verifier is trained using real solver predictions.
If the solver's candidate answer $\hat{a}$ exactly matches the gold answer $a^*$, the corresponding rollout is treated as a positive example labeled \texttt{YES}.
If the solver produces an incorrect answer, the rollout is treated as a negative example labeled \texttt{NO}.
This provides natural supervision from the solver's actual successes and failures without requiring manually constructed synthetic negatives.

The reward for training the verifier is defined as
\begin{equation}
r_V(q,\hat{a})
=
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\mathrm{pred}(\pi_V(q,\hat{a}))
=
\mathbf{1}[\hat{a}=a^*]
\right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{pred}(\cdot)$ extracts the binary \texttt{YES}/\texttt{NO} decision from the verifier output.
That is, the verifier receives reward $1$ if it outputs \texttt{YES} for a correct solver answer or \texttt{NO} for an incorrect solver answer.
It receives reward $0$ if it outputs \texttt{NO} for a correct answer or \texttt{YES} for an incorrect answer.

Trained in this way, the verifier learns to judge whether a question--answer pair has a recoverable hop structure.
In particular, the verifier does not simply imitate exact-match correctness; it learns whether there exists a reasoning path from the candidate answer back to the question conditions.
As a result, questions with broken logical connections, poor evidence alignment, or low answerability tend to receive lower verification scores.

\subsection{HRPO Proposer Training with Backward Verification}

The proposer $\pi_P$ is trained with HRPO as in DR-Zero.
Our modification is not to the HRPO optimization algorithm itself, but to the question-level reward used by HRPO.
In DR-Zero, the proposer reward is computed from the solver group's success rate on a generated question, yielding a difficulty-based reward.
We augment this reward with a backward verification term, so that the proposer is evaluated not only by whether its generated question has appropriate difficulty, but also by whether it is logically verifiable.

At iteration $t$, suppose the proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ generates a question $q$ from a document $d$:
\begin{equation}
q \sim \pi_P^{(t)}(\cdot \mid d).
\end{equation}
The solver $\pi_S^{(t)}$ then produces $K$ answer rollouts for $q$:
\begin{equation}
\{\hat{a}_i\}_{i=1}^{K} \sim \pi_S^{(t)}(\cdot \mid q).
\end{equation}
We define the correctness indicator for each answer rollout as
\begin{equation}
c_i = \mathbf{1}[\hat{a}_i = a^*].
\end{equation}
The average solver success rate for the question is
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{solve}}^{(t)}(q)
=
\frac{1}{K}
\sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i.
\end{equation}

The original HRPO objective in DR-Zero computes a difficulty reward $R_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(t)}(q)$ from this success rate.
This reward assigns low values to questions that all solvers answer easily or that no solver can answer, and high values to questions with intermediate success rates.

We add a backward verification reward $R_{\mathrm{bv}}^{(t)}(q)$.
This reward is computed post hoc after the solver attempts the generated question.
For each correct rollout, the verifier $\pi_V^{(t)}$ checks whether the candidate answer $\hat{a}_i$ can be connected back to the starting conditions and evidence chain of $q$ through reverse reasoning.
We define
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{bv}}^{(t)}(q)
=
\frac{1}{\max(1,\sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i)}
\sum_{i=1}^{K}
c_i \cdot
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\mathrm{pred}(\pi_V^{(t)}(q,\hat{a}_i))
=
\texttt{YES}
\right].
\end{equation}

Thus, $R_{\mathrm{bv}}^{(t)}(q)$ is the fraction of correct solver rollouts that are successfully backward-verified.
If no rollout matches the gold answer, then $\sum_i c_i=0$, and we set $R_{\mathrm{bv}}^{(t)}(q)$ to $0$.
This prevents the proposer from receiving a verification bonus when no verifiable correct answer is available.

The final proposer reward used by HRPO is
\begin{equation}
R_P^{(t)}(q)
=
R_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(t)}(q)
+
\lambda R_{\mathrm{bv}}^{(t)}(q),
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ controls the relative strength of the backward verification reward.
This reward is not assigned immediately after question generation; it is a delayed reward assigned post hoc to the proposer rollout that produced the question, after solver and verifier evaluation.
HRPO then uses this delayed question-level reward to update $\pi_P^{(t)}$.

Alternatively, if we want to preserve the original difficulty reward more strictly and use verifiability only as a bonus, we can use a multiplicative reward-shaping form:
\begin{equation}
R_P^{(t)}(q)
=
R_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(t)}(q)
\cdot
\left(1+\lambda R_{\mathrm{bv}}^{(t)}(q)\right).
\end{equation}
Under this form, questions with low base difficulty reward do not receive large reward even if they are backward-verifiable.
Thus, questions that are too easy or too hard are still suppressed, while questions that are both appropriately difficult and backward-verifiable receive higher reward.
In our framework, the main principle is to preserve the HRPO difficulty signal while incorporating backward verifiability as an additional reward component.

\subsection{Overall Training Procedure}

The full algorithm repeats the above process for $T$ iterations.
First, the proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ receives a Wikipedia passage $d$ and generates a multi-hop question $q$.
The generated question is constructed from a starting entity and $n$ factual hops, with the answer being the entity reached at the final hop.

Next, the solver $\pi_S^{(t)}$ performs retrieval-augmented reasoning on the generated question $q$.
It produces multiple rollouts, each consisting of search queries, retrieved passages, intermediate reasoning, and a final answer.
The solver is updated with GRPO based on the correctness of these rollouts.

Then, the backward verifier $\pi_V^{(t)}$ is trained using the solver's candidate answers.
Answers that match the gold answer are used as \texttt{YES} examples, while incorrect answers are used as \texttt{NO} examples.
The verifier is trained to reconstruct a reverse hop chain from the candidate answer back to the starting conditions of the question.

Finally, the proposer reward is computed.
The original DR-Zero difficulty reward is computed from the solver group's success rate, while the backward verification reward is computed from whether correct rollouts are logically verifiable by the backward verifier.
The combined question-level reward $R_P^{(t)}(q)$ is then assigned post hoc to the proposer rollout that generated the question and is used to update the proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ with HRPO.

Through this iterative process, the three models improve through interaction.
The solver improves its retrieval-augmented multi-hop reasoning ability by training on challenging generated questions.
The verifier becomes better at judging the logical verifiability of question--answer pairs using real solver predictions.
At the same time, the proposer learns from verifier feedback to generate questions that are not merely hard enough to make the solver fail, but appropriately difficult, answerable, and grounded in coherent multi-hop evidence chains.


\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets and Evaluation}

We evaluate on seven QA benchmarks spanning a range of hop complexity.
For single-hop QA we use Natural Questions (NQ), TriviaQA, and PopQA.
For multi-hop QA we use HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, MuSiQue, and Bamboogle.
All results are reported with exact match (EM).
We do not fine-tune or adapt any model on the evaluation benchmarks; all training is conducted on the self-play corpus derived from Wikipedia passages.

\subsection{Implementation}

Our base model is Qwen2.5-3B, used as the initialization for the proposer, solver, and verifier.
Training runs for $T = 3$ iterations with GRPO (group size 5 for the solver and verifier, group size 1 with reward group size 5 for the proposer).
Retrieval uses an E5-base-v2 encoder over a Wikipedia passage index, served via a FAISS flat index.
At inference, the solver issues multiple search queries per question as needed; we report greedy decoding EM.
Full hyperparameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:impl}.

\subsection{Baselines}

IRCoT interleaves chain-of-thought reasoning with iterative retrieval using a fixed retriever.
RAG and SFT represent standard retrieval-augmented generation and supervised fine-tuning without self-play.
Search-R1 and R-Zero are search-augmented RL methods that train models to issue queries end-to-end, representing the strongest non-self-play RL baselines.
SQLM* is a data-free self-play method that iteratively generates and filters training data.
We additionally report the base DrZero self-play loop (proposer + solver only, without verifier or growth-aware reward) as our primary comparison point.

\subsection{Ablation}
A natural concern is whether the performance gains in our method simply reflect additional training compute: the verifier stage adds a full RL training run per iteration, so a fair comparison must control for this.
We construct a compute-matched baseline by extending the base DrZero loop with additional solver training steps equivalent in FLOPs to the verifier training we add.
If our gains were purely a function of more gradient updates, this extended DrZero baseline should close the gap.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows the results.
The compute-matched DrZero improves slightly over the original DrZero, but falls consistently short of our method on all multi-hop benchmarks, confirming that the gains are not explained by training quantity alone.
We further conduct an ablation on backward verification and find that removing it degrades performance, indicating that this mechanism contributes materially to the overall improvements.
Finally, replacing real solver predictions with synthetically shuffled negatives for verifier training reduces verifier classification accuracy and propagates degradation to the proposer's question quality signal.

\subsection{Proposer Question Quality Analysis}

A key claim of our method is that backward verification improves not only solver accuracy but also the intrinsic quality of proposer-generated questions.
We evaluate this directly using two complementary metrics applied to the challenger's generation rollouts.
Because iteration-1 training suffered from search tool instability that caused the proposer to converge to trivial 1-hop generation, we report both metrics on gen-data from iteration 2 onward, where training conditions are stable and multi-hop generation is meaningful.

\paragraph{LLM Judge (Logical Validity Rate).}
For each generated triple (question $q$, multi-hop trace, gold answer $a^*$), we query three independent LLM judges---GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Gemini 3.1 Pro---each with a structured rubric that asks:
(i)~Is each hop in the reasoning chain logically motivated and grounded in the retrieved evidence?
(ii)~Is multi-hop reasoning genuinely required to reach the answer, or could the answer be derived from a single document?
Each judge outputs a binary valid/invalid label, and we take the majority vote as the final decision.
We report the fraction of generated questions judged valid (\textbf{LLM-Valid}).

\paragraph{Hop Necessity Rate (HNR).}
We inspect each question's generation rollout.
During multi-hop generation, the proposer issues sequential search queries and accumulates retrieved passages $\mathcal{R}_1, \mathcal{R}_2, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_n$.
If the gold answer $a^*$ appears in the retrieved passages of any intermediate hop before the final one, the last hop is redundant: the question could have been answered without it, and the hop chain is not genuinely multi-hop.
Formally,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HNR}(Q)
= 1 - \frac{\bigl|\{q \in Q : a^* \in \mathcal{R}_{<n}(q)\}\bigr|}{|Q|},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}_{<n}(q) = \bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1} \mathcal{R}_k(q)$ denotes the union of passages retrieved before the final hop.
A higher HNR indicates that the proposer generates questions for which every hop is genuinely necessary.

Table~\ref{tab:quality} compares both metrics on gen-data from iterations 2 and 3.
Our method consistently achieves higher LLM-Valid and HNR than DrZero at both iterations, confirming that backward verification encourages the proposer to generate questions that are not only solver-challenging but also logically coherent and multi-hop necessary.

\subsection{Proposer Question Difficulty Distribution}
As training progresses across iterations, we track how the distribution of question difficulty shifts.
We measure difficulty by the solver's pass rate on each generated question: questions where the solver succeeds on all rollouts are trivially easy, those where it fails all rollouts are unanswerable, and questions in the intermediate range are productively difficult.
In the base DrZero loop, the fraction of intermediate-difficulty questions grows in early iterations but plateaus by iteration 2, suggesting the proposer converges to a fixed difficulty regime.
In our method, by contrast, the fraction of intermediate-difficulty questions remains higher across iterations, and the average difficulty of generated questions tracks the solver's improving capability frontier more closely.

\subsection{Verifier Accuracy Across Iterations}

We evaluate the verifier's YES/NO classification accuracy on held-out solver predictions at the end of each iteration.
The verifier is initialized from the challenger checkpoint at iteration 1 and warm-started from the previous iteration's checkpoint in subsequent iterations.
Verifier accuracy improves monotonically across iterations, indicating that the backward hop search strategy becomes more reliable as the verifier accumulates experience with the proposer's question structure.
We also observe that verifier accuracy correlates positively with solver EM on multi-hop benchmarks: iterations where the verifier achieves higher classification accuracy tend to yield larger solver EM gains in the following iteration, consistent with the hypothesis that a more accurate verifier provides a cleaner quality signal to the proposer.

\subsection{Case Study}

Figure 2 illustrates a representative backward verification trace.
Given the question ``What country is the birthplace of the CEO of [Company X]?'' and the solver's candidate answer ``South Korea'', the verifier begins from ``South Korea'' and issues a search query to identify the CEO of Company X who was born there, then verifies that this person is indeed the CEO of Company X, and finally confirms that Company X matches the entity in the question.
When the backward chain closes, the verifier correctly outputs YES.
In contrast, for an incorrect solver answer, the backward search fails to find a consistent factual chain, and the verifier outputs NO.
This structured backward reasoning provides a verification signal that goes beyond surface-level answer matching and encourages the proposer to generate questions whose hop chains are factually grounded and recoverable in both directions.

\section{Conclusion}
We have argued that the quality of the proposer is the primary bottleneck in multi-hop QA self-play, and proposed a mechanism to address it directly.
Backward verification trains a dedicated verifier to assess solver predictions by tracing hop chains in reverse, providing a structured quality signal that distinguishes well-formed multi-hop questions from malformed ones.
This mechanism produces consistent EM gains across seven benchmarks, with the largest improvements on multi-hop datasets where proposer quality has the greatest impact.
The ablation against a compute-matched baseline confirms that the gains reflect the method rather than additional training compute.

A key message of this work is that self-play training for QA benefits from investing in both sides of the proposer-solver loop.
Most prior work on search-augmented RL focuses on improving the solver given a fixed question distribution; our results suggest that jointly improving the proposer through richer training signals is a complementary and underexplored direction.
We expect this perspective to generalize beyond multi-hop QA to other domains where self-play is used to generate training data for reasoning agents.
