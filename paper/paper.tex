\begin{abstract}
  Self-play frameworks for multi-hop question answering aim to build automated curricula by jointly training a proposer, which generates challenging questions, and a solver, which answers them. However, existing approaches largely overlook a fundamental bottleneck: the quality of the reward signal provided to the proposer. When the proposer is rewarded only according to whether the solver can answer a question, it may learn to generate questions that are superficially difficult but logically incomplete, thereby limiting the solver’s improvement.
In this paper, we propose a backward verifier to improve proposer training. Given a candidate answer, the backward verifier is trained to trace the hop chain in reverse through retrieval-augmented reasoning and determine whether the original question can be logically reconstructed from the answer and supporting evidence. This provides an explicit consistency signal that penalizes questions whose reasoning chains cannot be recovered from their answers. By augmenting the proposer’s objective with this backward verification reward, our framework encourages the generation of questions that are not only difficult but also logically coherent and pedagogically useful. Experiments on multi-hop QA benchmarks show that our approach consistently trains stronger solvers than self-play baselines, improving exact match by [X]\%. Further analysis demonstrates that the proposed verifier leads to questions with more coherent hop structure and better evidence alignment, highlighting the importance of reward quality in self-play curriculum construction.

% 다중 홉 QA에서도 이러한 자체 플레이 방식이 적용되고 있다. 기존 연구들은 제안자가 문서에서 출발하여 여러 사실적 홉을 연결함으로써 다중 홉 질문을 생성하고, 해결자가 검색 기반 추론을 통해 해당 홉 체인을 복원하며 답을 찾도록 학습시킨다. 이 접근은 사람이 직접 다중 홉 질문을 작성해야 하는 비용을 줄이고, 학습 과정에서 보다 동적인 질문 분포를 만들 수 있다는 점에서 유망하다.

Recent progress in self-improving language models \cite{ahmadian2024back, Spiral, Evolvesearch, zhao2026absolute} suggests that models can improve not only by imitating human-labeled data, but also by generating and learning from their own training signals. Across domains such as mathematical reasoning \cite{huang2026rzero}, code generation, and question answering, reinforcement learning from self-generated rollouts has enabled models to explore solution strategies beyond those available through supervised imitation alone. For question answering, this raises a natural question: can a model construct its own curriculum of increasingly difficult questions?
This question is particularly important for multi-hop question answering (QA). Unlike single-hop QA, multi-hop QA requires a model to connect pieces of evidence distributed across multiple documents and derive an answer through a coherent reasoning chain. A useful training question, therefore, should not merely contain an answer. It should require the solver to recover a valid chain of evidence, while ensuring that the chain is actually grounded in the underlying corpus.
However, most existing QA training methods rely on fixed datasets constructed before training begins. The questions, answers, and supervision signals are predetermined, and the difficulty distribution remains static even as the model improves. As a result, the model cannot actively seek out new questions that expose its current weaknesses, and the curriculum cannot adapt to the solver’s evolving capabilities. This limitation is especially pronounced in multi-hop QA, where progress depends on training examples that are not only difficult, but also grounded in coherent evidence chains.

Self-play offers a natural alternative. In a self-play framework, a proposer generates questions and a solver learns to answer them through repeated interaction. Ideally, the proposer generates questions near the frontier of the solver’s current capability, and the solver improves by learning to answer them. This process forms an automated curriculum without requiring human-authored questions, allowing the training distribution to evolve as the solver improves.
Recent work \cite{drzero} has instantiated this idea for multi-hop QA. The proposer constructs multi-hop questions by chaining factual hops from source documents, and the solver answers them by issuing search queries to recover the underlying evidence chain. This approach reduces dependence on manually written multi-hop questions and enables a more dynamic question distribution. However, it also introduces a critical bottleneck: the quality of self-play training is bounded by the quality of the proposer.
Existing self-play methods often overlook the quality of the reward signal provided to the proposer. Many approaches reward generated questions primarily according to whether the solver answers them correctly. Yet solver correctness alone is an insufficient measure of question quality. A question that the solver fails to answer may be difficult because it requires genuine multi-hop reasoning, but it may also be difficult because it is logically incomplete, underspecified, weakly grounded, or effectively unanswerable.
These two forms of difficulty have very different consequences for learning. The former provides useful pressure on the solver and exposes its limitations. The latter provides little or no meaningful supervision, because no coherent reasoning path can reliably lead to the answer. Nevertheless, an answer-based reward does not distinguish between useful difficulty and spurious difficulty. As a result, the proposer may learn to generate questions that are superficially hard but logically defective, thereby degrading the curriculum and limiting solver improvement.

Our central claim is that self-play for multi-hop QA should optimize not merely for difficult question generation, but for verifiably difficult question generation. A good multi-hop question should challenge the solver, but its answer should also be recoverable through a coherent evidence chain. In other words, difficulty should arise from the need to connect multiple facts, not from ambiguity, missing evidence, or broken reasoning structure.
To this end, we propose a backward verifier for improving proposer training. Given a generated question and a candidate answer, the backward verifier performs retrieval-augmented reasoning in reverse: starting from the candidate answer, it traces the required hop chain backward toward the entities and constraints specified in the question. Rather than merely checking whether the candidate answer matches a gold string, the verifier evaluates whether the answer is logically supported by a recoverable multi-hop reasoning path.
This backward verification signal provides richer supervision for the proposer. Questions whose reasoning chains cannot be reconstructed receive low reward, while questions with coherent and evidence-grounded hop structures receive high reward. By augmenting answer-based rewards with backward verification, the proposer is encouraged to generate questions that are not only difficult for the solver, but also answerable, logically consistent, and useful for training.

We integrate the backward verifier into an iterative self-play pipeline. At each iteration, the proposer generates multi-hop questions, the solver answers them through retrieval-augmented reasoning, and the backward verifier evaluates whether the solver’s candidate answers can be traced back to the question constraints through valid evidence chains. The resulting verification signal is used to refine proposer training, improving the quality of questions generated in subsequent iterations. Through this feedback loop, the self-play curriculum becomes increasingly composed of questions that are challenging yet logically verifiable.
We evaluate our method on both single-hop and multi-hop QA benchmarks. Experiments show that improving proposer rewards through backward verification leads to stronger solvers than existing self-play baselines. Further analyses of generated questions demonstrate that our method improves evidence alignment, hop-chain coherence, and answerability. These results indicate that the key to effective self-play in multi-hop QA is not only training the solver, but also improving the quality of the question distribution on which the solver learns.


\section{Related Work}

Multi-hop question answering requires a model to retrieve and chain multiple pieces of evidence before arriving at a final answer.
Early work addressed this through supervised decomposition --- breaking a question into sub-questions and solving each independently --- or through iterative retrieval pipelines that alternately retrieve and reason over passages.
More recent methods train models to actively issue search queries within a reasoning chain, interleaving retrieval and generation across multiple turns.
Representative approaches such as IRCoT structure reasoning as an interleaved chain-of-thought and retrieval process, while search-augmented RL methods (e.g., Search-R1, R-Zero) train models end-to-end to decide when and what to search.
Despite strong empirical results, these methods operate over a fixed training distribution: the set of questions and their difficulty are determined once, before training begins, and do not adapt as the model improves.

Self-evolution refers to a family of methods in which a model generates its own training data, receives outcome-based feedback, and iteratively improves without human annotation.
In mathematical reasoning and code generation, this has produced remarkable results: models trained purely on self-generated rollouts, rewarded by a verifier, surpass those trained on large supervised datasets.
The self-play variant extends this idea to two-model systems --- a generator and a discriminator, or a proposer and a solver --- where both models co-evolve.
Applied to QA, self-play allows the proposer to continuously adapt question difficulty to the solver's current capability, creating a natural curriculum.
Recent work such as DrZero instantiates this loop for multi-hop QA: the proposer chains factual hops starting from a Wikipedia passage, and the solver trains to recover the answer via search.
A shared limitation across these works is that the proposer is trained with only a coarse difficulty signal (e.g., solver accuracy), and its quality is not explicitly optimized beyond that.

Curriculum learning --- presenting training examples in order of increasing difficulty --- has long been known to accelerate learning and improve generalization.
In the self-play setting, the ideal curriculum is one where question difficulty co-evolves with solver capability: questions that are too easy provide no learning signal, while questions that are too hard yield no reward.
Existing self-play methods approximate this by rewarding the proposer when a fixed fraction of solver attempts succeed.
However, this signal is noisy: a question may appear difficult for the current solver simply because it is malformed or unanswerable, not because it lies at the true capability frontier.
More precise difficulty estimation --- for instance, by tracking how solver performance on the same questions changes across iterations --- provides a cleaner curriculum signal.

Bidirectional reasoning uses the structure of a problem in both forward and backward directions to improve inference quality.
In multi-hop QA, forward reasoning follows the hop chain from the question's starting entity to the answer; backward reasoning starts from a candidate answer and traces hops in reverse to verify consistency with the question.
This idea connects to self-consistency methods and chain-of-thought verification, where a model checks its own answers by re-d5  eriving them from a different starting point.
Recent work on bidirectional training \cite{chen-etal-2025-reverse} (e.g., RevThink) shows that training on both directions simultaneously improves reasoning robustness.
We adapt this principle to the self-play setting: rather than training the proposer or solver bidirectionally, we train a dedicated verifier to perform backward hop search over solver predictions, providing a structured verification signal that is richer than binary exact-match feedback.

\section{Method}

We propose backward-verified proposer refinement for self-play multi-hop QA. Our goal is to improve the proposer reward signal without changing the solver training objective or introducing an additional inference-time QA agent. In the standard self-play loop, the proposer is trained to generate questions that are structurally valid and appropriately difficult for the solver. However, difficulty alone does not guarantee that a generated question is logically coherent or grounded in a recoverable multi-hop evidence chain.

To address this issue, we add a delayed backward verification stage after solver training. At each iteration, the proposer first generates a set of multi-hop questions and is updated using the standard hop-grouped relative policy optimization (HRPO) reward. The generated questions are then used to train the solver with group relative policy optimization (GRPO). Only after this solver update do we run backward verification on the solver's answer rollouts. The resulting verification signal is then used to perform an additional proposer update on the same generated question rollouts.

Thus, backward verification is not an immediate reward computed at question generation time. It is a delayed proposer-side feedback signal obtained after the solver has learned from the generated questions. This design lets the proposer receive feedback not only about whether its questions were syntactically valid and appropriately difficult, but also about whether the solver's learned answers can be traced back through coherent evidence chains.

\subsection{Self-play multi-hop QA setup}

We build on the self-play multi-hop QA setting of DR-Zero. Given a raw Wikipedia passage $d$, the proposer $\pi_P$ generates a multi-hop question $q$. The proposer begins from a starting entity in $d$ and constructs a chain of $n$ factual hops through retrieval. The gold answer $a^\ast$ is defined as the entity reached at the final hop. Therefore, the generated question is intended to require multi-hop reasoning rather than direct extraction from a single passage.

The solver $\pi_S$ receives the generated question $q$ and attempts to answer it through retrieval-augmented reasoning. Instead of answering in a single step, the solver issues search queries over multiple turns, observes retrieved passages, performs intermediate reasoning, and finally outputs a candidate answer $\hat{a}$. For each question, we sample $K$ solver rollouts:
\[
\{\hat{a}_i\}_{i=1}^{K} \sim \pi_S(\cdot \mid q).
\]
The correctness of each rollout is measured by exact match with the generated gold answer:
\[
c_i = \mathbb{I}[\hat{a}_i = a^\ast],
\qquad
k = \sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i.
\]
The solver is optimized with GRPO using rollout-level correctness rewards. The proposer is optimized with HRPO using question-level rewards.

\subsection{Standard Proposer and Solver Training}

We first follow the self-play training loop of Dr. Zero. At iteration $t$, the
proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ generates a batch of question--answer pairs from
Wikipedia passages using multi-turn search. Each generated pair consists of a
question $q$, a synthesized answer $a^\ast$, and an implicit hop structure
induced by the proposer's search trajectory. The generated question--answer
pairs form the proposer dataset $\mathcal{D}_P^{(t)}$.

The proposer is optimized with HRPO, which computes relative advantages among
generated questions with the same hop count. This hop-conditioned normalization
provides a lower-variance baseline for proposer updates while avoiding the
nested sampling cost of applying standard GRPO to proposer training.

The standard proposer reward consists of a format reward and a difficulty
reward:
\[
R_{P,\mathrm{std}}(q)
=
R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(q)
+
R_{\mathrm{diff}}(q).
\]
The format reward $R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(q)$ encourages structurally valid outputs,
such as valid tool usage and extractable question and answer fields. Since this
term only enforces parseability and structural validity, we keep it unchanged
in our method.

The difficulty reward is computed from the solver group's outcomes on the
generated question. For $K$ solver rollouts, let
\[
c_i = \mathbb{I}[\hat{a}_i = a^\ast],
\qquad
k = \sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i.
\]
Following Dr. Zero, the difficulty reward is
\[
R_{\mathrm{diff}}(q)
=
\mathbb{I}(0 < k < K)
\frac{K-k}{K-1}.
\]
This reward assigns zero reward when no solver rollout is correct, since the
question may be unsolvable for the current solver, and also assigns zero reward
when all rollouts are correct, since the question is trivial. Among solvable
non-trivial questions, the reward is largest when exactly one rollout succeeds
and decreases as more rollouts become correct.

After the proposer update, the generated dataset $\mathcal{D}_P^{(t)}$ is used
to train the solver. For each generated question $q$, the solver produces
multiple retrieval-augmented answer rollouts. Each rollout consists of search
queries, retrieved passages, intermediate reasoning, and a final answer. The
solver is updated with GRPO using answer correctness as the rollout-level
reward. This update teaches the solver to answer the current generated
questions and exposes whether the proposer-generated questions provide useful
training signal.

Backward verification is performed only after this solver update. Therefore,
the verification stage evaluates the solver's post-training answer rollouts on
the generated questions, rather than the proposer's raw output alone. The
resulting verification signal is then used as an additional delayed
proposer-side reward term in our method.


\subsection{Backward verification after solver training}

After the solver has been trained on the generated QA pairs, we run a
backward verification pass on the solver's answer rollouts. Given a generated
question $x_i$ and a solver-produced candidate answer $\hat{y}_{i,m}$, the
backward verifier evaluates whether $\hat{y}_{i,m}$ is a correct answer to
$x_i$. In contrast to the forward solver, which starts from the question and
attempts to produce an answer, the backward verifier starts from the candidate
answer and checks its consistency with the question.

We formulate backward verification as a binary answer-grading problem:
\[
v_{i,m}
=
V(x_i,\hat{y}_{i,m})
\in \{0,1\},
\]
where $v_{i,m}=1$ indicates that the verifier judges $\hat{y}_{i,m}$ as a
valid answer to $x_i$, and $v_{i,m}=0$ indicates that the verifier judges it as
incorrect.

Since the generated QA pair provides a synthesized gold answer $y_i$, we can
measure whether the verifier's judgment agrees with the actual correctness of
the solver rollout. Let
\[
c_{i,m}
=
\mathbb{I}(y_i=\hat{y}_{i,m})
\]
denote the correctness of the solver's candidate answer under exact match.
The grading quality of the backward verifier on a solver rollout is then
defined as
\[
g_{i,m}
=
\mathbb{I}(v_{i,m}=c_{i,m}).
\]
Thus, the verifier receives a positive signal when it correctly accepts a
correct solver answer or correctly rejects an incorrect solver answer.

For each generated QA pair, we aggregate the grading outcomes across the
solver's $n$ answer rollouts:
\[
r_{\mathrm{bv}}(x_i,y_i)
=
\frac{1}{n}
\sum_{m=1}^{n}
g_{i,m}
=
\frac{1}{n}
\sum_{m=1}^{n}
\mathbb{I}
\left(
V(x_i,\hat{y}_{i,m})
=
\mathbb{I}(y_i=\hat{y}_{i,m})
\right).
\]
This score measures how reliably the backward verifier can judge the solver's
answers for the generated question. A high value indicates that the generated
question admits stable answer verification: correct answers can be accepted and
incorrect answers can be rejected. A low value indicates that the question may
be ambiguous, poorly grounded, or difficult to grade consistently.

We use this grading-quality score as a delayed proposer-side reward signal.
The score is computed only after the solver has produced answer rollouts for
the generated question, and therefore it is not available at question
generation time. Instead, it is assigned retrospectively to the proposer rollout
that generated $(x_i,y_i)$.

\subsection{Backward-verified proposer refinement}

The standard HRPO reward uses the solver pass rate to encourage questions that
are solvable but non-trivial:
\[
r_i
=
r_{\mathrm{fmt}}(x_i,y_i)
+
r_{\mathrm{diff}}(y_i,\{\hat{y}_{i,m}\}_{m=1}^{n}).
\]
However, this reward does not explicitly measure whether the generated question
can be reliably graded from solver answers. To incorporate this property, we
use the backward verification score $r_{\mathrm{bv}}(x_i,y_i)$ as a delayed
proposer-side refinement signal.

The backward-verified proposer reward is defined as
\[
r_i^{\mathrm{bv}}
=
r_{\mathrm{fmt}}(x_i,y_i)
+
r_{\mathrm{diff}}(y_i,\{\hat{y}_{i,m}\}_{m=1}^{n})
\left(
1+\lambda r_{\mathrm{bv}}(x_i,y_i)
\right),
\]
where $\lambda$ controls the strength of the backward verification bonus.

This design preserves the original format reward and difficulty reward, while
giving additional weight to questions whose solver answers can be reliably
graded by backward verification. The verification term does not directly
reward easy questions. Since it only rescales the difficulty component,
questions that are solved by all rollouts or by no rollout still receive low
difficulty reward. Among appropriately difficult questions, those with more
reliable backward grading receive larger proposer reward.

For the refinement update, we compute hop-conditioned advantages from the
backward-verified rewards:
\[
A_{i,h}^{\mathrm{bv}}
=
\frac{
r_i^{\mathrm{bv}} - \mathbb{E}_{j\in I_h}[r_j^{\mathrm{bv}}]
}{
\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{j\in I_h}[r_j^{\mathrm{bv}}] + \delta}
}.
\]
The proposer is then updated with the same HRPO objective form, replacing
$A_{i,h}$ with $A_{i,h}^{\mathrm{bv}}$.

\subsection{Overall training procedure}

The full training procedure repeats for $T$ self-play iterations. At each iteration $t$, we perform four stages.

First, the proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ generates a dataset of multi-hop questions from Wikipedia passages. The proposer is updated with the standard HRPO reward:
\[
R_{P,\mathrm{std}}(q)
=
R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(q)
+
R_{\mathrm{diff}}(q).
\]

Second, the generated dataset is used to train the solver $\pi_S^{(t)}$ with GRPO. The solver produces retrieval-augmented rollouts and is updated according to answer correctness.

Third, after solver training, backward verification is applied to the solver's answer rollouts. For each generated question, we compute $R_{\mathrm{bv}}(q)$ by checking whether correct solver answers can be traced backward to the question constraints through retrieved evidence.

Fourth, the proposer is refined with an additional HRPO update using the backward-verified reward:
\[
R_{P,\mathrm{bv}}(q)
=
R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(q)
+
R_{\mathrm{diff}}(q)
\cdot
\left(1+\lambda R_{\mathrm{bv}}(q)\right).
\]

Through this delayed feedback loop, the solver improves by learning from generated multi-hop questions, and the proposer improves by receiving a stronger reward signal after the solver has attempted to learn from those questions. The resulting curriculum favors questions that are well formatted, appropriately difficult, and verifiable through coherent backward evidence reconstruction.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets and evaluation}

We evaluate on seven QA benchmarks spanning a range of hop complexity.
For single-hop QA we use Natural Questions (NQ) \cite{NQ}, TriviaQA \cite{TriviaQA}, and PopQA \cite{PopQA}.
For multi-hop QA we use HotpotQA \cite{HotpotQA}, 2WikiMultiHopQA \cite{MultiHopQA}, MuSiQue \cite{MuSiQue}, and Bamboogle \cite{CompositionalityGap}.
All results are reported with exact match (EM).
We do not fine-tune or adapt any model on the evaluation benchmarks; all training is conducted on the self-play corpus derived from Wikipedia passages.
Our base model is Qwen2.5-3B, used as the initialization for the proposer, solver, and verifier.
Training runs for $T = 3$ iterations with GRPO (group size 5 for the solver and verifier, group size 1 with reward group size 5 for the proposer).
Retrieval uses an E5-base-v2 encoder over a Wikipedia passage index, served via a FAISS flat index.
At inference, the solver issues multiple search queries per question as needed; we report greedy decoding EM.

\subsection{Baselines}

IRCoT interleaves chain-of-thought reasoning with iterative retrieval using a fixed retriever.
RAG and SFT represent standard retrieval-augmented generation and supervised fine-tuning without self-play.
Search-R1 and R-Zero are search-augmented RL methods that train models to issue queries end-to-end, representing the strongest non-self-play RL baselines.
SQLM* is a data-free self-play method that iteratively generates and filters training data.
We additionally report the base DrZero self-play loop (proposer + solver only, without verifier or growth-aware reward) as our primary comparison point.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Main Results}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports exact match (EM) results on seven QA benchmarks,
covering both single-hop and multi-hop question answering. Our method achieves
the best average performance among all compared methods, improving over the
base DrZero self-play baseline from 0.326 to 0.349 average EM. The gains are
consistent across all benchmarks, indicating that backward verification provides
a useful additional training signal for self-play QA.

The improvements are most pronounced on multi-hop benchmarks. Compared with
DrZero, our method improves from 0.298 to 0.315 on HotpotQA, from 0.291 to
0.300 on 2WikiMultiHopQA, from 0.091 to 0.105 on MuSiQue, and from 0.200 to
0.230 on Bamboogle. These results support our hypothesis that proposer quality
is especially important in multi-hop QA, where the solver's learning signal
depends heavily on whether generated questions are coherent, non-trivial, and
grounded in recoverable reasoning structure.

On single-hop benchmarks, the gains are smaller but still consistent. Our
method improves over DrZero from 0.397 to 0.425 on NQ, from 0.572 to 0.610 on
TriviaQA, and from 0.431 to 0.455 on PopQA. This suggests that backward
verification does not harm single-hop generalization, even though its main
benefit is expected in settings that require multi-step reasoning.

Compared with Search-R1~\cite{SearchR1}, the strongest non-self-play
search-augmented RL baseline in our evaluation, our method achieves a higher

\subsection{Proposer Question Quality Analysis}

A central claim of our method is that backward verification improves the
quality of the questions generated by the proposer, rather than merely
providing additional optimization steps. We therefore evaluate proposer question
quality directly using two complementary metrics: LLM-based validity scoring and
hop necessity rate (HNR).

\paragraph{LLM judge.}
For each generated triple consisting of a question $q$, a multi-hop generation
trace, and a synthesized gold answer $a^\ast$, we query three independent LLM
judges: GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Gemini 3.1 Pro. Each judge is given a
structured rubric that asks whether each hop in the reasoning chain is
logically motivated and grounded in retrieved evidence, and whether multi-hop
reasoning is genuinely required to reach the answer. Each judge assigns a
validity score in $[0,1]$, where a higher score indicates a more logically
valid and genuinely multi-hop question. We report the average validity score
from each judge and the mean score across the three judges.

\paragraph{Hop necessity rate.}
LLM-based judging captures logical validity, but it may not directly measure
whether every generated hop is necessary. We therefore also compute hop
necessity rate (HNR) from the proposer's generation rollout. During multi-hop
generation, the proposer issues sequential search queries and accumulates
retrieved passages $\mathcal{R}_1,\mathcal{R}_2,\ldots,\mathcal{R}_n$. If the
gold answer $a^\ast$ appears in any retrieved passage before the final hop, then
the final hop is redundant: the question could have been answered earlier, and
the generated chain is not genuinely multi-hop.

Formally, for a set of generated questions $Q$, we define
\[
\mathrm{HNR}(Q)
=
1 -
\frac{
\left|\{q\in Q : a^\ast \in \mathcal{R}_{<n}(q)\}\right|
}{
|Q|
},
\]
\[
\mathcal{R}_{<n}(q)
=
\bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1}
\mathcal{R}_k(q)
\]
denotes the union of passages retrieved before the final hop. A higher HNR
indicates that the proposer generates questions whose final hop is genuinely
necessary.

Table~\ref{tab:quality} reports proposer question quality on generated data.
Our method achieves higher LLM validity scores and higher HNR than DrZero,
showing that backward verification improves not only downstream solver
accuracy, but also the intrinsic quality of the generated training questions.
These results support the view that the verifier provides a useful
proposer-side signal: it encourages questions that are logically coherent,
grounded in evidence, and genuinely multi-hop rather than merely difficult for
the current solver.

\subsection{Ablation}

A natural concern is whether the performance gains in our method simply reflect
additional training compute. Since our method introduces an additional verifier
training stage, a fair comparison must control for the extra optimization budget.
We therefore construct a compute-matched baseline by extending the base DrZero
loop with additional solver training steps whose FLOPs match the verifier
training cost in our method. If the gains were purely due to more gradient
updates, this compute-matched DrZero baseline should close the gap.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the ablation results. The compute-matched
DrZero baseline improves slightly over the original DrZero, but still falls
consistently short of our method. This suggests that the gains are not explained
by additional training compute alone.

We further ablate the construction of verifier training data. In our full
method, the verifier is trained on real solver rollouts, so both positive and
negative examples reflect the solver's actual answer distribution. In
particular, the negative examples correspond to realistic solver mistakes rather
than arbitrary mismatched answers. As an alternative, we construct a
shuffled-negative variant, where negative answers are obtained by replacing the
solver prediction with an answer sampled from another QA pair in the same batch.
This synthetic construction is cheaper and simpler, but it removes the natural
error patterns produced by the solver.

The shuffled-negative variant performs worse than the full method, indicating
that realistic solver-generated mistakes provide a more useful training signal
for the verifier. This result supports our design choice of training the
verifier on the full solver rollout distribution rather than on artificial
answer mismatches.

\section{Conclusion}
We have argued that the quality of the proposer is the primary bottleneck in multi-hop QA self-play, and proposed a mechanism to address it directly.
Backward verification trains a dedicated verifier to assess solver predictions by tracing hop chains in reverse, providing a structured quality signal that distinguishes well-formed multi-hop questions from malformed ones.
This mechanism produces consistent EM gains across seven benchmarks, with the largest improvements on multi-hop datasets where proposer quality has the greatest impact.
The ablation against a compute-matched baseline confirms that the gains reflect the method rather than additional training compute.

A key message of this work is that self-play training for QA benefits from investing in both sides of the proposer-solver loop.
Most prior work on search-augmented RL focuses on improving the solver given a fixed question distribution; our results suggest that jointly improving the proposer through richer training signals is a complementary and underexplored direction.
We expect this perspective to generalize beyond multi-hop QA to other domains where self-play is used to generate training data for reasoning agents.

\section{Proposer Prompt}
\label{app:proposer_prompt}

The proposer is initialized with the following system prompt.
The placeholders \texttt{\{hops\}} and \texttt{\{document\}} are substituted at generation time with the target hop count and the Wikipedia source passage, respectively.

\small
\begin{verbatim}
You are an expert in question generation. Craft one challenging,
deterministic question and its single, unambiguous answer based on
the provided source document. The logical path must start from the
document and require exactly n hops (i.e., n-1 searches) to reach
the final answer.

### Definitions
1. Hop: A node in the reasoning chain. Hop 1 is the starting entity
   found in the document. Hop n is the final answer.

### Inputs
1. n: the exact number of hops in the reasoning chain.
2. Source document: the full source text.

### Process & Tools
1. Analyze the Document and Select the Starting Point
   - Select a specific entity explicitly mentioned in the text.
     This entity becomes Hop 1 (the initial clue).
2. Design the Chain Forwards
   - From Hop 1 to Hop 2: Identify a factual relation of Hop 1 that
     is NOT in the text but can be found via search. Result is Hop 2.
   - Iterate: Continue connecting Hop i to Hop i+1 using verifiable
     relations found via search.
   - Stop at Hop n. Hop n must be a single, canonical final answer.
3. Reasoning & Search Protocol
   - Always reason inside <think> ... </think>.
   - Issue each search query via <tool_call> ... </tool_call>.
   - Results appear in <tool_response> ... </tool_response>.
4. Output Format (example for n = 3, i.e., 2 searches):
   <think> Reasoning step 1: Identify Hop 1, formulate query </think>
   <tool_call> [Query to find Hop 2] </tool_call>
   <think> Reasoning step 2: Identify Hop 2, formulate query </think>
   <tool_call> [Query to find Hop 3] </tool_call>
   <think> Final: Confirm Hop 3, formulate question </think>
   <question> [Question starting from Hop 1, asking for Hop 3] </question>
   <answer> [Hop 3] </answer>

### Critical Rules
1. Hop 1 must be explicitly present in the source text.
2. Each link beyond Hop 1 must use the search engine.
3. Emit exactly (n-1) tool_call entries, no more, no fewer.
4. The question mentions only Hop 1; do not hint at intermediate hops.
5. Each hop must depend strictly on its immediate predecessor.

Generate a question with n = {hops} hops from: {document}
\end{verbatim}
\normalsize

\section{Backward Verifier Prompts}
\label{app:verifier_prompts}

\subsection{Inference Prompt}
\label{app:verifier_inference}

At inference time the backward verifier receives the generated question
and a candidate solver answer, and traces the hop chain in reverse
through retrieval.
The verifier outputs \texttt{<answer>YES</answer>} or
\texttt{<answer>NO</answer>} to indicate whether the candidate answer
is consistent with the question.

\small
\begin{verbatim}
You are verifying whether a candidate answer correctly answers a
multi-hop question. Verify by reasoning BACKWARD: start from the
candidate answer and trace each hop in reverse using search, until
you reach the starting entity stated in the question.
Always reason inside <think> and </think>. For each backward hop,
call a search engine by <tool_call> query </tool_call> and results
appear between <tool_response> and </tool_response>. After tracing
all hops backward, output <answer>YES</answer> if the candidate
answer is correct, or <answer>NO</answer> if it is incorrect.
Question: {question}  Candidate Answer: {candidate_answer}
\end{verbatim}
\normalsize

\subsection{Training Prompt}
\label{app:verifier_training}

The verifier model is trained with GRPO on solver rollouts.
Each training instance uses the following prompt, paired with a binary
ground-truth label (\texttt{yes} if the solver answer matches the gold
answer, \texttt{no} otherwise).

\small
\begin{verbatim}
You are given a question and a proposed answer. You must conduct
reasoning inside <think> and </think> first every time you get new
information. After reasoning, if you need to look up information to
verify the answer, you can call a search engine by
<tool_call> query </tool_call> and it will return the top searched
results between <tool_response> and </tool_response>. You can search
as many times as you want. After verification, determine whether
the proposed answer is correct and output your judgment as
<answer>yes</answer> or <answer>no</answer>.

Question: {question}
Proposed answer: {proposed_answer}
\end{verbatim}
\normalsize

\section{LLM Judge Prompts}

Each judge receives the following system and user prompts and returns a

\subsection{System Prompt}

\small

You are an expert evaluator of multi-hop reasoning questions.

Respond ONLY with a JSON object -- no markdown, no explanation
outside JSON.
\end{verbatim}
\normalsize

\subsection{User Prompt Template}

The placeholders \texttt{\{question\}} and \texttt{\{answer\}} are filled
with the generated question and its synthesized gold answer.

\small
\begin{verbatim}
Rate the overall quality of the following question and its
ground-truth answer.

Question: {question}
Ground-truth Answer: {answer}

A high-quality multi-hop question satisfies all of:
- It is grounded in real, verifiable facts and the answer is correct.
- The answer can be logically derived by following the reasoning
  chain in the question.
- It genuinely requires at least two reasoning steps (not solvable
  by a single fact lookup).

Give a single score from 0.0 to 1.0:
  1.0 -- all three properties fully satisfied
  0.5 -- partially satisfied (e.g., factually ok but only single-hop)
  0.0 -- fails on most or all properties

Respond with exactly this JSON:
{
  "score": <float between 0.0 and 1.0>,
  "reasoning": "<one sentence explaining the score>"
}
\end{verbatim}
\normalsize

passages retrieved at the \emph{final} hop $\mathcal{R}_n$, and not in
any earlier retrieved set $\mathcal{R}_{<n}$.
A question fails HNR when the answer is already recoverable from an
intermediate retrieval step, indicating that the final hop is redundant.
Figure~\ref{fig:hnr_examples} illustrates both outcomes with concrete
3-hop examples (requiring 2 searches each).
