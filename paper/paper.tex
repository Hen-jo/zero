\begin{abstract}
  Self-play frameworks for multi-hop question answering aim to build automated curricula by jointly training a proposer, which generates challenging questions, and a solver, which answers them. However, existing approaches largely overlook a fundamental bottleneck: the quality of the reward signal provided to the proposer. When the proposer is rewarded only according to whether the solver can answer a question, it may learn to generate questions that are superficially difficult but logically incomplete, thereby limiting the solver’s improvement.
In this paper, we propose a backward verifier to improve proposer training. Given a candidate answer, the backward verifier is trained to trace the hop chain in reverse through retrieval-augmented reasoning and determine whether the original question can be logically reconstructed from the answer and supporting evidence. This provides an explicit consistency signal that penalizes questions whose reasoning chains cannot be recovered from their answers. By augmenting the proposer’s objective with this backward verification reward, our framework encourages the generation of questions that are not only difficult but also logically coherent and pedagogically useful. Experiments on multi-hop QA benchmarks show that our approach consistently trains stronger solvers than self-play baselines, improving exact match by 5\%. Further analysis demonstrates that the proposed verifier leads to questions with more coherent hop structure and better evidence alignment, highlighting the importance of reward quality in self-play curriculum construction.

\section{Introduction}

Recent progress in self-improving language models \cite{ahmadian2024back, Spiral, Evolvesearch, zhao2026absolute, wang2025socratic} suggests that models can improve not only by imitating human-labeled data, but also by generating and learning from their own training signals. This idea has been successfully explored in domains such as mathematical reasoning \cite{huang2026rzero}, code generation \cite{wei2025toward}, and question answering \cite{chen2025self}, where reinforcement learning on self-generated rollouts enables models to explore strategies beyond those available through supervised imitation alone. In question answering, this naturally raises the possibility of constructing an adaptive curriculum of increasingly challenging questions.
This motivation is particularly compelling in multi-hop question answering (QA), where answering a question requires connecting evidence scattered across multiple documents through a coherent reasoning chain. Unlike single-hop QA, a useful multi-hop training question must do more than admit an answer: it must require the solver to recover a valid and corpus-grounded chain of evidence. However, most existing QA training methods rely on fixed datasets prepared before training begins, with predetermined questions, answers, and supervision signals. As the model improves, the training distribution remains static, preventing the curriculum from adapting to the solver's evolving weaknesses.

Self-play offers a natural alternative. In a self-play framework, a proposer generates questions and a solver learns to answer them through repeated interaction. Ideally, the proposer generates questions near the frontier of the solver's current ability, allowing the solver to improve without relying on manually authored curricula. Recent work \cite{drzero} extends this idea to multi-hop QA by having the proposer compose questions from factual hops in source documents and the solver answer them by reconstructing the underlying evidence chain.
However, directly extending self-play to multi-hop QA introduces a key challenge: the effectiveness of training depends critically on how the proposer is optimized. Existing self-play methods typically reward generated questions based on whether the solver answers them correctly. Yet in multi-hop QA, solver accuracy alone is insufficient to assess question quality. A question that the solver fails to answer may indeed require genuine multi-hop reasoning, but it may also be difficult because it is logically incomplete, weakly grounded, or effectively unanswerable. Moreover, when hop counts are predetermined during question generation, imprecise question construction can introduce unnecessary hops, yielding questions that only superficially appear multi-hop without genuinely requiring the intended reasoning depth.
These issues reveal that the main limitation of existing self-play methods lies not only in solver training, but in proposer training. If the proposer is rewarded solely based on solver failure, it may learn to generate questions that are superficially difficult while lacking logical validity, evidence grounding, or fidelity to the intended hop structure. We therefore argue that proposer training in multi-hop QA should optimize not merely for difficulty, but for \emph{verifiably difficult} question generation---that is, for questions that are challenging for the solver while remaining answerable through coherent, evidence-grounded, and hop-faithful reasoning chains.

To realize this objective, we propose a backward verifier that scores generated question-answer pairs and provides a verification-based reward signal for proposer training. Given a generated question and a candidate answer, the verifier performs retrieval-augmented reasoning in reverse: starting from the candidate answer, it traces the required evidence chain backward toward the entities and constraints specified in the question. Rather than only checking whether the candidate answer matches a target string, the verifier evaluates whether the answer is supported by a recoverable multi-hop reasoning path that satisfies the question constraints. This verification signal assigns low reward to question-answer pairs whose reasoning chains are irrecoverable, weakly grounded, or inconsistent with the intended hop structure, and high reward to pairs with coherent and evidence-grounded reasoning chains.
We integrate the backward verifier into an iterative self-play pipeline. At each iteration, the proposer generates multi-hop questions, the solver answers them through retrieval-augmented reasoning, and the backward verifier evaluates the resulting question-answer pairs to refine proposer training. Through this feedback loop, the self-play curriculum is progressively shifted toward questions that are not only challenging, but also logically verifiable.

We evaluate our method on both single-hop and multi-hop QA benchmarks. Experiments show that improving proposer rewards through backward verification yields stronger solvers than existing self-play baselines. Beyond downstream solver accuracy, we also directly evaluate the quality of proposer-generated questions using LLM-based validity scoring and hop necessity rate (HNR). These analyses show that our method produces questions that are more logically valid and genuinely multi-hop, confirming that backward verification improves not only solver learning outcomes, but also the intrinsic quality of the training questions themselves. Taken together, these results suggest that effective self-play in multi-hop QA depends not only on training the solver, but also on improving the quality of the question distribution from which the solver learns.




\section{Related Work}

Multi-hop question answering (QA) requires models to retrieve and integrate multiple pieces of evidence before producing an answer \cite{MuSiQue}. Earlier approaches often relied on supervised question decomposition or iterative retrieval pipelines, whereas more recent methods interleave reasoning and search by enabling models to generate retrieval queries during reasoning. For example, IRCoT \cite{trivedi2023interleaving} combines chain-of-thought reasoning with retrieval, while search-augmented reinforcement learning methods \cite{li2025search, jiang2025s3} such as Search-R1 \cite{SearchR1} and R-Zero \cite{huang2026rzero} train models end-to-end to make search decisions as part of generation. Our work also considers multi-hop QA in a search-based setting, but focuses more specifically on how training questions are generated and selected during learning.

A related line of work studies self-evolution and self-play \cite{chen2024self}, where models improve using self-generated data and outcome-based feedback rather than human annotation. In QA, this often takes the form of jointly training a proposer and a solver, allowing the training distribution to evolve with the solver's capability and naturally inducing a curriculum. DrZero is the closest example in this setting: it trains a proposer to generate factual hop chains from Wikipedia passages and a solver to answer them through search. Our work builds on this proposer--solver framework, but extends it by introducing additional verification signals for proposer training.

This setting is also closely connected to curriculum learning, which aims to present examples at an appropriate level of difficulty. In self-play QA, prior work \cite{wang2025socratic, huang2026rzero} commonly approximates this objective by rewarding questions that are solved by some, but not all, solver rollouts, thereby favoring examples of intermediate difficulty. While this heuristic is useful, it does not explicitly distinguish between informative difficulty and difficulty caused by ambiguity, weak grounding, or unreliable answerability. Our method is related to this curriculum-based perspective, while providing an explicit mechanism for evaluating question quality beyond solver pass rates.

Our approach is further related to work on bidirectional reasoning and verification. In multi-hop QA, forward reasoning follows a chain from the question to the answer, whereas backward verification starts from a candidate answer and checks whether it is supported by the question and evidence. This perspective is related to self-consistency, chain-of-thought verification, and bidirectional reasoning methods such as RevThink~\cite{chen-etal-2025-reverse}. Unlike prior approaches that apply bidirectional training directly to the main reasoning model, our work introduces a dedicated verifier over solver predictions and uses its backward verification signal to improve proposer training.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We propose backward-verified proposer refinement for self-play multi-hop QA. Our goal is to improve the proposer reward signal without changing the solver training objective. In the standard self-play loop, the proposer is trained to generate questions that are structurally valid and appropriately difficult for the solver. However, particularly in multi-hop tasks, difficulty rewards alone make it difficult to guarantee that the generated problems are logically consistent and based on a recoverable multi-hop evidence chain.

To address this issue, we add a delayed backward verification stage after solver training. At each iteration, the proposer first generates a set of multi-hop questions and is updated using the standard hop-grouped relative policy optimization (HRPO) reward. The generated questions are then used to train the solver with group relative policy optimization (GRPO). Only after this solver update do we run backward verification on the solver's answer rollouts. The resulting verification signal is then used to perform an additional proposer update on the same generated question rollouts.

Thus, backward verification is not an immediate reward computed at question generation time. It is a delayed proposer-side feedback signal obtained after the solver has learned from the generated questions. This design lets the proposer receive feedback not only about whether its questions were syntactically valid and appropriately difficult, but also about whether the solver's learned answers can be traced back through coherent evidence chains.

\subsection{Self-play multi-hop QA setup}

We build on the self-play multi-hop QA setting of DR-Zero \cite{drzero}. Given a raw Wikipedia passage $d$, the proposer $\pi_P$ generates a multi-hop question $q$. Starting from an entity in $d$, the proposer constructs a chain of $n$ factual hops through retrieval, and the gold answer $a^\ast$ is defined as the entity reached at the final hop. The generated question is therefore intended to require multi-hop reasoning rather than direct extraction from a single passage.

Given the generated question $q$, the solver $\pi_S$ attempts to answer it through retrieval-augmented reasoning. Instead of answering in a single step, the solver issues search queries over multiple turns, observes retrieved passages, performs intermediate reasoning, and finally outputs a candidate answer. For each generated question, we sample multiple solver rollouts to assess how the current solver responds to the proposer's question distribution. These rollouts are used in the standard self-play training loop described next.


\subsection{Standard proposer and solver training}

We first follow the self-play training loop of Dr. Zero. At iteration $t$, the
proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ generates a batch of question--answer pairs from
Wikipedia passages using multi-turn search. Each generated pair consists of a
question $q$, a synthesized answer $a^\ast$, and an implicit hop structure
induced by the proposer's search trajectory. The generated question--answer
pairs form the proposer dataset $\mathcal{D}_P^{(t)}$.

The proposer is optimized with HRPO, which computes relative advantages among
generated questions with the same hop count. This hop-conditioned normalization
provides a lower-variance baseline for proposer updates while avoiding the
nested sampling cost of applying standard GRPO to proposer training.

The standard proposer reward consists of a format reward and a difficulty
reward:
\[
R_{P,\mathrm{std}}(q)
=
R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(q)
+
R_{\mathrm{diff}}(q).
\]
The format reward $R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(q)$ encourages structurally valid outputs,
such as valid tool usage and extractable question and answer fields. Since this
term only enforces parseability and structural validity, we keep it unchanged
in our method.

The difficulty reward is computed from the solver group's outcomes on the
generated question. For $K$ solver rollouts, let $c_i = \mathbb{I}[\hat{a}_i = a^\ast]$ and $k = \sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i$; following Dr. Zero, the difficulty reward is
\[
R_{\mathrm{diff}}(q) = \mathbb{I}(0 < k < K)\,\frac{K-k}{K-1}.
\]
This reward assigns zero reward when no solver rollout is correct, since the
question may be unsolvable for the current solver, and also assigns zero reward
when all rollouts are correct, since the question is trivial. Among solvable
non-trivial questions, the reward is largest when exactly one rollout succeeds
and decreases as more rollouts become correct.

After the proposer update, the generated dataset $\mathcal{D}_P^{(t)}$ is used
to train the solver. For each generated question $q$, the solver produces
multiple retrieval-augmented answer rollouts. Each rollout consists of search
queries, retrieved passages, intermediate reasoning, and a final answer. The
solver is updated with GRPO using answer correctness as the rollout-level
reward. This update teaches the solver to answer the current generated
questions and exposes whether the proposer-generated questions provide useful
training signal.

Backward verification is performed only after this solver update. Therefore,
the verification stage evaluates the solver's post-training answer rollouts on
the generated questions, rather than the proposer's raw output alone. The
resulting verification signal is then used as an additional delayed
proposer-side reward term in our method.


\subsection{Backward verification after solver training}

After the solver has been trained on the generated QA pairs, we run a
backward verification pass on the solver's answer rollouts. Given a generated
question $x_i$ and a solver-produced candidate answer $\hat{y}_{i,m}$, the
backward verifier evaluates whether $\hat{y}_{i,m}$ is a correct answer to
$x_i$. In contrast to the forward solver, which starts from the question and
attempts to produce an answer, the backward verifier starts from the candidate
answer and checks its consistency with the question.

Concretely, the verifier performs retrieval-augmented reasoning in the backward direction.
Treating the candidate answer as the final hop, it issues sequential search queries to retrieve supporting evidence, tracing the evidence chain in reverse hop-by-hop until reaching the starting entity specified in the question.
This backward search process is the verifier's core inference mechanism: its yes/no judgment is grounded in retrieved evidence, not in direct string matching against a reference answer.

For training, we use EM-based labels as supervision: a verifier output of \texttt{yes} is correct when the candidate matches the synthesized gold answer $y_i$, and \texttt{no} when it does not.
These labels teach the verifier to produce correct backward-retrieval judgments; the distinction is that EM serves as the \emph{training signal}, while the verifier's decision process at inference relies on the backward evidence chain it constructs through retrieval.

We formulate backward verification as a binary answer-grading problem:
\[
v_{i,m}
=
V(x_i,\hat{y}_{i,m})
\in \{0,1\},
\]
where $v_{i,m}=1$ indicates that the verifier judges $\hat{y}_{i,m}$ as a
valid answer to $x_i$, and $v_{i,m}=0$ indicates that the verifier judges it as
incorrect.

Since the generated QA pair provides a synthesized gold answer $y_i$, we can
measure whether the verifier's judgment agrees with the actual correctness of
the solver rollout. Let
\[
c_{i,m}
=
\mathbb{I}(y_i=\hat{y}_{i,m})
\]
denote the correctness of the solver's candidate answer under exact match.
The grading quality of the backward verifier on a solver rollout is then
defined as
\[
g_{i,m}
=
\mathbb{I}(v_{i,m}=c_{i,m}).
\]
Thus, the verifier receives a positive signal when it correctly accepts a
correct solver answer or correctly rejects an incorrect solver answer.

For each generated QA pair, we aggregate the grading outcomes across the
solver's $K$ answer rollouts:
\[
r_{\mathrm{bv}}(x_i,y_i)
=
\frac{1}{K}
\sum_{m=1}^{K}
g_{i,m}
=
\frac{1}{K}
\sum_{m=1}^{K}
\mathbb{I}
\left(
V(x_i,\hat{y}_{i,m})
=
\mathbb{I}(y_i=\hat{y}_{i,m})
\right).
\]
This score measures how reliably the backward verifier can judge the solver's
answers for the generated question. A high value indicates that the generated
question admits stable answer verification: correct answers can be accepted and
incorrect answers can be rejected. A low value indicates that the question may
be ambiguous, poorly grounded, or difficult to grade consistently.

We use this grading-quality score as a delayed proposer-side reward signal.
The score is computed only after the solver has produced answer rollouts for
the generated question, and therefore it is not available at question
generation time. Instead, it is assigned retrospectively to the proposer rollout
that generated $(x_i,y_i)$.

\subsection{Backward-verified proposer refinement}

The standard HRPO reward uses the solver pass rate to encourage questions that
are solvable but non-trivial:
\[
r_i
=
r_{\mathrm{fmt}}(x_i,y_i)
+
r_{\mathrm{diff}}(y_i,\{\hat{y}_{i,m}\}_{m=1}^{K}).
\]
However, this reward does not explicitly measure whether the generated question
can be reliably graded from solver answers. To incorporate this property, we
use the backward verification score $r_{\mathrm{bv}}(x_i,y_i)$ as a delayed
proposer-side refinement signal.

The backward-verified proposer reward is defined as
\[
r_i^{\mathrm{bv}}
=
r_{\mathrm{fmt}}(x_i,y_i)
+
r_{\mathrm{diff}}(y_i,\{\hat{y}_{i,m}\}_{m=1}^{K})
\left(
1+r_{\mathrm{bv}}(x_i,y_i)
\right).
\]

This design preserves the original format reward and difficulty reward, while
giving additional weight to questions whose solver answers can be reliably
graded by backward verification. The verification term does not directly
reward easy questions. Since it only rescales the difficulty component,
questions that are solved by all rollouts or by no rollout still receive low
difficulty reward. Among appropriately difficult questions, those with more
reliable backward grading receive larger proposer reward.

For the refinement update, we compute hop-conditioned advantages from the
backward-verified rewards:
\[
A_{i,h}^{\mathrm{bv}}
=
\frac{
r_i^{\mathrm{bv}} - \mathbb{E}_{j\in I_h}[r_j^{\mathrm{bv}}]
}{
\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{j\in I_h}[r_j^{\mathrm{bv}}] + \delta}
},
\]
where $I_h = \{j : n_j = h\}$ denotes the index set of generated questions
with hop count $h$, and $\delta$ is a small constant for numerical stability.
The proposer is then updated with the same HRPO objective form, replacing
$A_{i,h}$ with $A_{i,h}^{\mathrm{bv}}$.

\subsection{Overall training procedure}

The full training procedure repeats for $T$ self-play iterations. At each iteration $t$, we perform four stages.

First, the proposer $\pi_P^{(t)}$ generates a dataset of multi-hop questions from Wikipedia passages. The proposer is updated with the standard HRPO reward:
\[
R_{P,\mathrm{std}}(q)
=
R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(q)
+
R_{\mathrm{diff}}(q).
\]

Second, the generated dataset is used to train the solver $\pi_S^{(t)}$ with GRPO. The solver produces retrieval-augmented rollouts and is updated according to answer correctness.

Third, after solver training, backward verification is applied to the solver's answer rollouts. For each generated question, we compute $R_{\mathrm{bv}}(q)$ by checking whether correct solver answers can be traced backward to the question constraints through retrieved evidence.

Fourth, the proposer is refined with an additional HRPO update using the backward-verified reward:
\[
R_{P,\mathrm{bv}}(q)
=
R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(q)
+
R_{\mathrm{diff}}(q)
\cdot
\left(1+R_{\mathrm{bv}}(q)\right).
\]

Through this delayed feedback loop, the solver improves by learning from generated multi-hop questions, and the proposer improves by receiving a stronger reward signal after the solver has attempted to learn from those questions. The resulting curriculum favors questions that are well formatted, appropriately difficult, and verifiable through coherent backward evidence reconstruction.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets and evaluation}

We evaluate on seven QA benchmarks spanning a range of hop complexity.
For single-hop QA we use Natural Questions (NQ) \cite{NQ}, TriviaQA \cite{TriviaQA}, and PopQA \cite{PopQA}.
For multi-hop QA we use HotpotQA \cite{HotpotQA}, 2WikiMultiHopQA \cite{MultiHopQA}, MuSiQue \cite{MuSiQue}, and Bamboogle \cite{CompositionalityGap}.
All results are reported with exact match (EM).
We do not fine-tune or adapt any model on the evaluation benchmarks; all training is conducted on the self-play corpus derived from Wikipedia passages.
Our base model is Qwen2.5-3B, used as the initialization for the proposer, solver, and verifier.
Training runs for $T = 3$ iterations. Retrieval uses an E5-base-v2 encoder over a Wikipedia passage index, served via a FAISS flat index.
At inference, the solver issues multiple search queries per question as needed; we report greedy decoding EM.

\subsection{Baselines}

IRCoT interleaves chain-of-thought reasoning with iterative retrieval using a fixed retriever.
RAG and SFT represent standard retrieval-augmented generation and supervised fine-tuning without self-play.
Search-R1 and R-Zero are search-augmented RL methods that train models to issue queries end-to-end, representing the strongest non-self-play RL baselines.
Self-questioning language models (SQLM) \cite{chen2025self} is a data-free self-play method that iteratively generates and filters training data.
We additionally report the base DrZero self-play loop (proposer + solver only, without verifier or growth-aware reward) as our primary comparison point.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Main Results}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports exact match (EM) results on seven QA benchmarks,
covering both single-hop and multi-hop question answering. Our method achieves
the best average performance among all compared methods, improving over the
base DrZero self-play baseline from 0.326 to 0.343 average EM. The gains are
consistent across all benchmarks, indicating that backward verification provides
a useful additional training signal for self-play QA.
Note that our method uses 400 total training steps across three iterations, compared to 300 for the base DrZero baseline, as the verifier training stage adds 100 additional steps over the standard proposer--solver loop.

The improvements are most pronounced on multi-hop benchmarks. Compared with
DrZero, our method improves from 0.298 to 0.304 on HotpotQA, from 0.291 to
0.319 on 2WikiMultiHopQA, from 0.091 to 0.105 on MuSiQue, and from 0.200 to
0.240 on Bamboogle. These results support our hypothesis that proposer quality
is especially important in multi-hop QA, where the solver's learning signal
depends heavily on whether generated questions are coherent, non-trivial, and
grounded in recoverable reasoning structure.

On single-hop benchmarks, the gains are smaller but still consistent. Our
method improves over DrZero from 0.397 to 0.410 on NQ, from 0.572 to 0.584 on
TriviaQA, and from 0.431 to 0.441 on PopQA. This suggests that backward
verification does not harm single-hop generalization, even though its main
benefit is expected in settings that require multi-step reasoning.

Compared with Search-R1~\cite{SearchR1}, the strongest non-self-play
search-augmented RL baseline in our evaluation, our method achieves a higher
average EM. This indicates that improving the proposer in an iterative
self-play loop can provide generalization benefits beyond training the solver
on a fixed question distribution.

We further examine learning dynamics across self-play iterations in
Table~\ref{tab:dynamics}. DrZero improves over early iterations but begins to
saturate by iteration 3. In contrast, our method obtains a larger improvement by
iteration 2, reaching 0.344 average EM. This early gain suggests that backward
verification provides a stronger proposer-side signal, allowing the generated
training curriculum to better track the solver's improving capability.

All reported numbers are averaged over three random seeds. The relative ordering of methods was consistent across seeds, supporting the reliability of the observed gains.

\subsection{Proposer Question Quality Analysis}

A central claim of our method is that backward verification improves the
quality of the questions generated by the proposer, rather than merely
providing additional optimization steps. We therefore evaluate proposer question
quality directly using two complementary metrics: LLM-based validity scoring and
hop necessity rate (HNR).

\paragraph{LLM judge.}
For each generated triple consisting of a question $q$, a multi-hop generation
trace, and a synthesized gold answer $a^\ast$, we query three independent LLM
judges: GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Gemini 3.1 Pro. Each judge is given a
structured rubric that asks whether each hop in the reasoning chain is
logically motivated and grounded in retrieved evidence, and whether multi-hop
reasoning is genuinely required to reach the answer. Each judge assigns a
validity score in $[0,1]$, where a higher score indicates a more logically
valid and genuinely multi-hop question. We report the average validity score
from each judge and the mean score across the three judges.

\paragraph{Hop necessity rate.}
LLM-based judging captures logical validity, but it may not directly measure
whether every generated hop is necessary. We therefore also compute hop
necessity rate (HNR) from the proposer's generation rollout. During multi-hop
generation, the proposer issues sequential search queries and accumulates
retrieved passages $\mathcal{R}_1,\mathcal{R}_2,\ldots,\mathcal{R}_n$. If the
gold answer $a^\ast$ appears in any retrieved passage before the final hop, then
the final hop is redundant: the question could have been answered earlier, and
the generated chain is not genuinely multi-hop.

Formally, for a set of generated questions $Q$, we define
\[
\mathrm{HNR}(Q)
=
1 -
\frac{
\left|\{q\in Q : a^\ast \in \mathcal{R}_{<n}(q)\}\right|
}{
|Q|
},
\qquad
\mathcal{R}_{<n}(q)
=
\bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1}
\mathcal{R}_k(q).
\]
Here, $\mathcal{R}_{<n}(q)$ denotes the union of passages retrieved before the
final hop. A higher HNR indicates that the proposer generates questions whose
final hop is genuinely necessary.
Table~\ref{tab:quality} reports proposer question quality on generated data.
Our method achieves higher LLM validity scores and higher HNR than DrZero,
showing that backward verification improves not only downstream solver
accuracy, but also the intrinsic quality of the generated training questions.
These results support the view that the verifier provides a useful
proposer-side signal: it encourages questions that are logically coherent,
grounded in evidence, and genuinely multi-hop rather than merely difficult for
the current solver.

\subsection{Ablation}

A natural concern is whether the performance gains in our method simply reflect
additional training compute. Since our method introduces an additional verifier
training stage, a fair comparison must control for the extra optimization budget.
We therefore construct a compute-matched baseline by extending the base DrZero
loop with additional solver training steps whose FLOPs match the verifier
training cost in our method. If the gains were purely due to more gradient
updates, this compute-matched DrZero baseline should close the gap.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the ablation results. The compute-matched
DrZero baseline falls slightly below the original DrZero, and consistently falls
short of our method. This suggests that the gains are not explained
by additional training compute alone.

We further ablate the construction of verifier training data. In our full
method, the verifier is trained on real solver rollouts, so both positive and
negative examples reflect the solver's actual answer distribution. In
particular, the negative examples correspond to realistic solver mistakes rather
than arbitrary mismatched answers. As an alternative, we construct a
shuffled-negative variant, where negative answers are obtained by replacing the
solver prediction with an answer sampled from another QA pair in the same batch.
This synthetic construction is cheaper and simpler, but it removes the natural
error patterns produced by the solver.

The shuffled-negative variant performs worse than the full method, indicating
that realistic solver-generated mistakes provide a more useful training signal
for the verifier. This result supports our design choice of training the
verifier on the full solver rollout distribution rather than on artificial
answer mismatches.

\section{Conclusion}
We have argued that the quality of the proposer is the primary bottleneck in multi-hop QA self-play, and proposed a mechanism to address it directly.
Backward verification trains a dedicated verifier to assess solver predictions by tracing hop chains in reverse, providing a structured quality signal that distinguishes well-formed multi-hop questions from malformed ones.
This mechanism produces consistent EM gains across seven benchmarks, with the largest improvements on multi-hop datasets where proposer quality has the greatest impact.
The ablation against a compute-matched baseline confirms that the gains reflect the method rather than additional training compute.

A key message of this work is that self-play training for QA benefits from investing in both sides of the proposer-solver loop.
Most prior work on search-augmented RL focuses on improving the solver given a fixed question distribution; our results suggest that jointly improving the proposer through richer training signals is a complementary and underexplored direction.
We expect this perspective to generalize beyond multi-hop QA to other domains where self-play is used to generate training data for reasoning agents.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The primary limitation of our approach is increased computational cost.
Introducing the backward verifier adds a third GRPO training stage to each self-play iteration, increasing total training compute relative to the two-stage proposer--solver loop in the base DrZero framework.
In our experiments each iteration trains three models for 50 steps each, roughly 1.5$\times$ the compute of the verifier-free baseline.
Future work could explore more lightweight verification alternatives---such as reward-model distillation or rule-based approximations---that preserve the quality signal while reducing training overhead.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{app:implementation}

We implement our method as an alternating optimization of the proposer, solver, and backward verifier, following the self-play loop described in Section~\ref{sec:method}.
All experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40\,GB VRAM each).

\paragraph{Proposer training.}
The proposer is optimized with HRPO for 50 steps per iteration.
We roll out one response per prompt and extract the corresponding question and answer, which are passed to the solver to compute the difficulty reward.
The proposer uses a default generation ratio of 4:3:2:1 for 1-, 2-, 3-, and 4-hop questions, respectively.
The format reward comprises four components---adherence to the \texttt{<think>} structure, valid tool usage, an extractable \texttt{<question>} field, and an extractable \texttt{<answer>} field---each contributing equally up to a total maximum of 0.5, with the difficulty score adding up to 1.0 on top.
Key HRPO hyperparameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:hrpo_hp}.

\paragraph{Solver training.}
The solver is trained with GRPO for 50 steps per iteration, using group size 5.
Key GRPO hyperparameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:grpo_hp}.

\paragraph{Verifier training.}
The backward verifier is trained with HRPO for 50 steps per iteration, using group size 1.
Each training instance consists of a generated question paired with a solver candidate answer; the binary label is \texttt{yes} if the candidate matches the gold answer and \texttt{no} otherwise.
The verifier shares the same optimizer and scheduler settings as the solver; its hyperparameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:verifier_hp}.

\paragraph{Retrieval.}
The external retriever indexes the corpus and embeds queries using the E5-base model.
At inference time, an approximate nearest-neighbor search retrieves the top-3 passages, which are formatted and returned as the tool response.
Both training and evaluation use exact match as the instance-level score.

\paragraph{Iterations.}
Solver performance typically peaks after 2--3 self-evolution iterations; we therefore train for 3 iterations (150 steps per model), which is significantly fewer than non-self-play baselines such as Search-R1.

\section{Proposer prompt}
\label{app:proposer_prompt}

The proposer takes as input a target hop count $n$ and a source document $d$.
Its goal is to generate one deterministic multi-hop question $q$ with a single unambiguous gold answer $a^\ast$.
The intended reasoning chain follows the structure $\texttt{Hop\,1} \to \texttt{Search} \to \texttt{Hop\,2} \to \cdots \to \texttt{Search} \to \texttt{Hop\,}n$, where the final hop constitutes the answer.
Three structural constraints govern generation:
(i) Hop~1 must be explicitly present in the source document;
(ii) every subsequent hop must be obtained through a search engine call; and
(iii) the proposer must emit exactly $n-1$ tool calls, while the question text refers only to Hop~1 and does not reveal intermediate hops.

The placeholders \texttt{\{hops\}} and \texttt{\{document\}} are substituted at generation time
with the target hop count and the Wikipedia source passage, respectively.

\begin{tcolorbox}[promptbox, title={Proposer System Prompt}, breakable]
\small
\begin{verbatim}
You are an expert in question generation. Craft one challenging,
deterministic question and its single, unambiguous answer based on
the provided source document. The logical path must start from the
document and require exactly n hops (i.e., n-1 searches) to reach
the final answer.

### Definitions
1. Hop: A node in the reasoning chain. Hop 1 is the starting entity
   found in the document. Hop n is the final answer.

### Inputs
1. n: the exact number of hops in the reasoning chain.
2. Source document: the full source text.

### Process & Tools
1. Analyze the Document and Select the Starting Point
   - Select a specific entity explicitly mentioned in the text.
     This entity becomes Hop 1 (the initial clue).
2. Design the Chain Forwards
   - From Hop 1 to Hop 2: Identify a factual relation of Hop 1 that
     is NOT in the text but can be found via search. Result is Hop 2.
   - Iterate: Continue connecting Hop i to Hop i+1 using verifiable
     relations found via search.
   - Stop at Hop n. Hop n must be a single, canonical final answer.
3. Reasoning & Search Protocol
   - Always reason inside <think> ... </think>.
   - Issue each search query via <tool_call> ... </tool_call>.
   - Results appear in <tool_response> ... </tool_response>.
4. Output Format (example for n = 3, i.e., 2 searches):
   <think> Reasoning step 1: Identify Hop 1, formulate query </think>
   <tool_call> [Query to find Hop 2] </tool_call>
   <think> Reasoning step 2: Identify Hop 2, formulate query </think>
   <tool_call> [Query to find Hop 3] </tool_call>
   <think> Final: Confirm Hop 3, formulate question </think>
   <question> [Question starting from Hop 1, asking for Hop 3] </question>
   <answer> [Hop 3] </answer>

### Critical Rules
1. Hop 1 must be explicitly present in the source text.
2. Each link beyond Hop 1 must use the search engine.
3. Emit exactly (n-1) tool_call entries, no more, no fewer.
4. The question mentions only Hop 1; do not hint at intermediate hops.
5. Each hop must depend strictly on its immediate predecessor.

Generate a question with n = {hops} hops from: {document}
\end{verbatim}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Backward Verifier Prompts}
\label{app:verifier_prompts}

The verifier is trained with HRPO on solver rollouts. Each training instance
uses the following prompt, paired with a binary ground-truth label:
\texttt{yes} if the solver answer matches the gold answer, and \texttt{no}
otherwise.

\begin{tcolorbox}[verifybox, title={Backward Verifier Training Prompt}, breakable]
\small
\begin{verbatim}
You are an expert answer verifier for multi-hop questions. Given a
question and a proposed answer, determine whether the proposed answer
is correct by reasoning BACKWARD through the evidence chain.

### Definitions
1. Hop: A node in the multi-hop reasoning chain. Hop 1 is the
   starting entity in the question. Hop n is the final answer.
2. Backward Verification: Starting from the proposed answer (Hop n),
   trace the chain in reverse toward Hop 1 using search.

### Inputs
1. Question: a multi-hop question whose answer requires following a
   chain of factual hops from a starting entity.
2. Proposed answer: a candidate answer to evaluate.

### Process & Tools
1. Identify the Proposed Answer as the Final Hop
   - Treat the proposed answer as Hop n.
2. Trace the Chain in Reverse
   - From Hop n backward: search for a fact that links the proposed
     answer toward the intermediate hops.
   - Iterate: continue tracing backward until you reach Hop 1,
     the starting entity stated in the question.
3. Reasoning & Search Protocol
   - Always reason inside <think> ... </think>.
   - Issue each search query via <tool_call> ... </tool_call>.
   - Results appear in <tool_response> ... </tool_response>.
4. Output Format (example for a 2-hop chain):
   <think> The proposed answer is X; search backward to find what
   connects X to the question's starting entity. </think>
   <tool_call> [Query to find the intermediate connection] </tool_call>
   <think> The result confirms / does not confirm the link to the
   starting entity. </think>
   <answer>yes</answer>   (or <answer>no</answer>)

### Critical Rules
1. Always start reasoning from the proposed answer, not the question.
2. Use the search engine to verify each backward hop.
3. Output <answer>yes</answer> if the proposed answer is correct,
   or <answer>no</answer> if it is incorrect.
4. Base your judgment on retrieved evidence, not assumption.

Question: {question}
Proposed answer: {proposed_answer}
\end{verbatim}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{LLM Judge Prompts}
\label{app:judge_prompts}

Each generated question is scored by three independent LLM judges.
The judge receives the question, the gold answer, the proposer's generation trace (including retrieved passages), and the target hop count.
Four criteria are evaluated: (i) \textbf{Factual correctness}---whether the answer is real and verifiable, consistent with the retrieved passages; (ii) \textbf{Chain validity}---whether each search step is logically motivated and connected to the previous hop; (iii) \textbf{Hop necessity}---whether the gold answer appears only in the final retrieved passage, not in any intermediate one; and (iv) \textbf{Hop count compliance}---whether the reasoning chain uses exactly the instructed number of hops.
Each judge returns a scalar validity score $s_{\mathrm{judge}} \in \{0.0,\, 0.2,\, 0.5,\, 0.7,\, 1.0\}$, where higher scores indicate more coherent, grounded, and genuinely multi-hop questions.

\subsection{System Prompt}

\begin{tcolorbox}[judgebox, title={LLM Judge System Prompt}, breakable]
\small
\begin{verbatim}
You are an expert evaluator of multi-hop reasoning questions.
Your task is to judge the overall quality of a question that was
automatically generated to require multi-step (multi-hop) reasoning.

Respond ONLY with a JSON object -- no markdown, no explanation
outside JSON.
\end{verbatim}
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{User Prompt Template}

The placeholders \texttt{\{question\}}, \texttt{\{answer\}}, \texttt{\{hop\_n\}},
\texttt{\{n\_searches\}}, and \texttt{\{trace\_block\}} are filled with the
generated question, its synthesized gold answer, the target hop count, the
number of required searches, and the proposer's full generation trace
(interleaved search queries and retrieved passages), respectively.
When the generation trace is unavailable, a simplified fallback prompt
omitting \texttt{\{trace\_block\}} is used.

\begin{tcolorbox}[judgebox, title={LLM Judge User Prompt}, breakable]
\small
\begin{verbatim}
Rate the overall quality of the following automatically generated
multi-hop question.

=== Question ===
{question}

=== Ground-truth Answer ===
{answer}

=== Generation Trace (proposer's actual search process) ===
Hop instruction: {hop_n}-hop question (the proposer was asked to
build a {hop_n}-step reasoning chain requiring {n_searches} search(es))

{trace_block}

=== Evaluation Criteria ===
A high-quality multi-hop question satisfies ALL of the following:

1. Factual correctness: The answer is real and verifiable,
   consistent with the retrieved passages above.
2. Chain validity: Each search step is logically motivated --
   it follows from the previous hop and moves toward the answer.
3. Hop necessity: The gold answer does NOT appear in any intermediate
   retrieved passage (only in the final one). If the answer already
   appears in an earlier passage, the later hops are redundant.
4. Hop count compliance: The reasoning chain uses exactly {hop_n}
   hops as instructed (neither fewer nor more).

=== Scoring ===
  1.0 -- all four criteria fully satisfied
  0.7 -- mostly satisfied; minor issue
  0.5 -- partially satisfied (e.g., answer leaks in an intermediate
         step, or wrong hop count, but chain is otherwise logical)
  0.2 -- mostly fails (e.g., answer reachable in 1 step, or chain
         is circular/incoherent)
  0.0 -- fails on most or all criteria

Respond with exactly this JSON:
{
  "score": <float between 0.0 and 1.0>,
  "reasoning": "<one or two sentences explaining which criteria
                pass/fail>"
}
\end{verbatim}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Hop Necessity Rate: Illustrative Examples}
\label{app:hnr_examples}

HNR measures whether the gold answer $a^\ast$ appears
exclusively in the passages retrieved at the final hop $\mathcal{R}_n$, and not
in any earlier retrieved set $\mathcal{R}_{<n}$. A question fails HNR when the
answer is already recoverable from an intermediate retrieval step, indicating
that the final hop is redundant.

\centering
\begin{tcolorbox}[appbox,title={Hop Necessity Rate: Illustrative Examples},width=\textwidth]
\small

\begin{tcolorbox}[judgebox,title={HNR Pass: Genuine 3-Hop Chain}]
\small
\textbf{Source entity:} The Colosseum \quad|\quad \textbf{Target hops:} 3 \quad|\quad \textbf{Gold answer:} \textit{Falacrine}

\smallskip
\textbf{Generated question:} \emph{What was the birthplace of the Roman emperor who commissioned the Colosseum?}

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.10\linewidth}p{0.84\linewidth}@{}}
\textbf{Search 1} & \texttt{who commissioned the Colosseum} \\[1mm]
$\mathcal{R}_1$ &
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}
\small\itshape
``The Colosseum, also known as the Flavian Amphitheatre, was commissioned by Emperor \textbf{Vespasian} of the Flavian dynasty around AD 72. Construction began under Vespasian's reign and was completed by his son Titus in AD 80\ldots''
\end{minipage}\\[3mm]
\textbf{Search 2} & \texttt{Vespasian birthplace} \\[1mm]
$\mathcal{R}_2$ &
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}
\small\itshape
``Vespasian (9 November AD 9 -- 23 June AD 79) was Roman emperor from AD 69 to 79 and the founder of the Flavian dynasty. He was born in \textbf{\underline{Falacrine}}, a small village in the Sabine Hills near Reate (modern-day Rieti), in central Italy\ldots''
\end{minipage}\\
\end{tabular}

\medskip
\textbf{HNR check:} \emph{Falacrine} $\notin \mathcal{R}_1$ \;\textbf{and}\; \emph{Falacrine} $\in \mathcal{R}_2$ \quad$\Rightarrow$\quad \textcolor{green!50!black}{\textbf{Pass.}}
The gold answer does not appear in any intermediate passage---it is revealed only at the final retrieval step. Both hops are genuinely necessary.
\end{tcolorbox}

\medskip

\begin{tcolorbox}[failbox,title={HNR Fail \#1: Answer Leaked in First Retrieval (3-hop chain)}]
\small
\textbf{Source entity:} Leonardo da Vinci \quad|\quad \textbf{Target hops:} 3 \quad|\quad \textbf{Gold answer:} \textit{Paris}

\smallskip
\textbf{Generated question:} \emph{In which city is the museum that houses the most famous painting by the artist who painted the Last Supper?}

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.10\linewidth}p{0.84\linewidth}@{}}
\textbf{Search 1} & \texttt{Leonardo da Vinci most famous painting} \\[1mm]
$\mathcal{R}_1$ &
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}
\small\itshape
``Leonardo da Vinci's most celebrated work is the \textit{Mona Lisa} (c.\,1503--1519), \textbf{which has been on permanent display at the Louvre Museum in \underline{Paris}, France}, since 1797. The painting was acquired by King Francis I of France and later became part of the French royal collection\ldots''
\end{minipage}\\[3mm]
\textbf{Search 2} & \texttt{where is the Mona Lisa exhibited} \\[1mm]
$\mathcal{R}_2$ &
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}
\small\itshape
``The Mona Lisa is currently exhibited in the Salle des États (Room 711) of the Louvre Museum, located in the 1st arrondissement of Paris\ldots''
\end{minipage}\\
\end{tabular}

\medskip
\textbf{HNR check:} \emph{Paris} $\in \mathcal{R}_1$ \quad$\Rightarrow$\quad \textcolor{red!70!black}{\textbf{Fail.}}
The gold answer is already present in the \emph{first} retrieved passage. The proposer intended a 3-hop chain (da Vinci $\to$ Mona Lisa $\to$ Louvre $\to$ Paris), but the answer \emph{Paris} leaked into $\mathcal{R}_1$, making Search 2 entirely redundant.
\end{tcolorbox}
