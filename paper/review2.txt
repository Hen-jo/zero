Don't Just Ask—Verify: Rethinking Reward Signals for Self-Play Multi-hop QA

Summary
This paper tackles a key bottleneck in self-play for multi-hop question answering: weak proposer rewards that conflate “hard” with “ill-posed.” The authors introduce a backward verifier that, after solver training, evaluates whether solver answers can be consistently validated via reverse, retrieval-augmented reasoning, and uses this delayed verification score to reweight the proposer’s difficulty reward. Across seven QA benchmarks using a 3B LLM and Wikipedia retrieval, the method yields consistent (though modest) EM gains over a strong self-play baseline (DrZero) and improves intrinsic question-quality metrics such as hop necessity.

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation
Positions verification as a proposer-side reward, not only as solver-side supervision, addressing a genuine gap in self-play curricula for multi-hop QA.
Introduces a delayed, backward-verification signal that rescales difficulty rewards rather than replacing them, mitigating trivialization or reward hacking.
Trains the verifier on real solver rollouts, which is a sensible design to capture realistic error distributions.
Experimental rigor and validation
Evaluates on seven benchmarks spanning single- and multi-hop tasks using a single backbone and fixed retrieval setup.
Includes compute-matched ablation to control for added optimization steps and an ablation on negative construction (real rollouts vs. shuffled negatives).
Tracks proposal quality via hop necessity rate and an LLM-judge rubric, going beyond solely downstream QA metrics.
Clarity of presentation
The self-play loop (proposer, solver, and delayed verifier refinement) is clearly stated, with careful motivation for why solver pass rate alone is insufficient.
Reward shaping choice—multiplicative reweighting of difficulty by the verification score—has an intuitive rationale tied to preserving curriculum shape.
Significance of contributions
Addresses a practically important failure mode in self-play multi-hop QA: generating superficially hard but logically incoherent questions.
The approach is complementary to search-augmented RL and could generalize to other self-play settings where data quality is critical.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns
The description of how the verifier itself is trained is underspecified. While the paper states the verifier uses retrieval-augmented reasoning and HRPO, it lacks concrete details (format, actions, reward, and labels) that tie the binary decision V(x, ŷ) to an explicit training objective.
The proposed verification reward r_bv is defined as agreement with exact-match correctness across solver rollouts. This couples verifier “quality” to EM, which may penalize semantically correct but string-mismatched answers and conflates question verifiability with surface-matching reliability.
The reweighting r_diff · (1 + r_bv) is reasonable but somewhat ad hoc; there is limited analysis of sensitivity to this design compared to alternatives (e.g., additive bonus, thresholded gating, or direct forward-path consistency scores).
Experimental gaps or methodological issues
Reported EM gains are modest overall; without visible error bars or run-to-run variances in the main tables, it is hard to assess statistical significance.
The LLM-judge validity table shows only iteration-1 scores; later-iteration judge scores are not reported (marked as “—”), weakening the claim that logical validity consistently improves across training.
The compute-matched baseline adds solver steps but does not match “where” compute is spent (e.g., extra proposer updates without verification, or an alternative verifier-free proposer signal), making it difficult to isolate that the benefit derives specifically from backward verification rather than simply from an extra proposer update.
Clarity or presentation issues
Inconsistency between text and Table 1 on the average EM improvement (text mentions 0.349, table shows 0.343).
The verifier’s operational details (prompting format, tool usage constraints, acceptance criteria) are not fully described in the main paper; readers must infer much from high-level statements.
Missing related work or comparisons
The bidirectional/verification literature is acknowledged, but closer contrasts to verification-as-reward designs (e.g., judge-based faithfulness rewards in multi-hop RAG) and to recent self-play frameworks that incorporate verification gates would sharpen positioning.
A baseline using a forward verification (evidence/path completeness) or a simple rule-based grader would help demonstrate that the “backward” direction is the key ingredient.
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation
The delayed verification signal is a clear and defensible way to improve proposer optimization without changing solver objectives. Rescaling difficulty by a verifiability measure aligns with the aim to discourage ill-posed or weakly grounded questions.
However, the definition of r_bv as verifier/EM agreement introduces a proxy mismatch: it measures grading consistency against EM, not necessarily backward reconstructibility of reasoning chains. If correct answers are paraphrased or partially correct, the signal may be noisy. More direct path consistency measures (e.g., verification that retrieved evidence satisfies all question constraints) would better align with the stated goal.
The verifier’s training remains a gray box. Since it is purportedly an agent performing retrieval-augmented reverse reasoning and yielding a binary verdict, the paper should specify its observation/action space, format checks, and reward mapping (e.g., supervised labels versus RL with sparse rewards). Without this, reproducibility and interpretability of V are weakened.
Experimental evaluation assessment
Breadth of benchmarks and consistency of gains are positive. Improvements are largest where they matter (multi-hop, especially Bamboogle and 2Wiki). Single-hop scores do not regress.
The compute-matched control is a thoughtful inclusion, but a stronger control would equalize proposer-side updates in a verifier-free setting (e.g., an extra proposer-HRPO step with a proxy signal or uniform reweighting) to isolate the benefit of verification per se.
The LLM-judge and HNR analyses are useful. Yet the lack of later-iteration LLM-judge numbers limits the strength of the “improved logical validity” claim. Additionally, HNR, as defined by surface presence of a* in earlier hops, can be brittle (answers may appear early in contexts that do not logically entail the final answer).
No variance/error bars are visible in the main tables; given the small gains on some datasets, showing run statistics is important.
Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)
Relative to self-play and verification-heavy frameworks (e.g., SSP’s RAG verification gate or judge-based faithfulness rewards like in CRAFT), this work’s main novelty is using a dedicated backward verifier to shape the proposer’s reward after solver training. That differentiates it from gating acceptance or purely forward/judge-only rewards.
Compared with iterative RAG methods (IRCoT, EKA, CoopRAG, RISE, PAR-RAG), the focus here is not the solver’s reasoning policy per se but the evolving data distribution via proposer incentives. This angle is underexplored and complementary.
Closer empirical or conceptual comparisons to judge-based faithfulness (e.g., CRAFT-like faithfulness reward components) or to self-play frameworks with integrated verification (e.g., SSP) would strengthen the positioning and clarify where backward verification provides unique benefits.
Discussion of broader impact and significance
Highlighting proposer reward quality is impactful: many self-play curricula fail by drifting toward unproductive “hard cases.” Encouraging verifiability can yield better training signals and more robust solvers.
Risks include overfitting to verifier idiosyncrasies and subtle coupling to exact-match correctness, potentially biasing against semantically correct paraphrases. There is also increased compute overhead; although the authors acknowledge this, further efficiency strategies (e.g., verifier distillation) would be valuable.
The general idea may transfer to other agentic domains (e.g., coding, tool use) where reverse-checking or consistency verification is feasible.
Questions for Authors
How exactly is the verifier trained? Please detail its action/observation space, prompt/format, reward function, and whether it uses supervised labels (from EM and evidence) or RL with HRPO. What constitutes a “group” for HRPO when group size is 1?
Why define r_bv via agreement with EM rather than a direct evidence/path-consistency score (e.g., verifying that constraints are satisfied and citations support each hop)? Did you test alternative verification metrics?
Can you report error bars or standard deviations for the main results across independent runs? Several gains are small and would benefit from significance reporting.
The LLM-judge validity scores for iterations 2 and 3 are missing in Table 2. Can you provide those numbers and describe the judging rubric in the main text (or summarize from the appendix)?
Did you try an ablation that adds an extra proposer update without using verification (e.g., uniform reweighting or random reweighting of difficulty) to disentangle “more proposer updates” from “verification-driven updates”?
How sensitive are results to the multiplicative reweighting choice r_diff · (1 + r_bv)? Please provide a sensitivity study (e.g., λ in 1 + λ r_bv, additive bonuses, thresholded gating).
Does the verifier ever accept semantically correct but EM-mismatched answers? If so, how does that affect training (since g = I(v = c) would penalize such cases)? Would a semantic matching metric for c (e.g., answer normalization or an LLM judge) improve robustness?
Can you provide qualitative examples where backward verification clearly distinguishes between ill-posed vs. well-posed multi-hop questions, and show how proposer generations change over iterations?
Overall Assessment
This paper advances self-play for multi-hop QA by focusing on proposer reward quality via a backward, delayed verification signal. The idea is timely and well-motivated, the pipeline is plausible, and the empirical results show consistent, if modest, gains across a diverse benchmark suite alongside improvements in a proxy for hop necessity. The study is strengthened by compute-matched and data-construction ablations. However, the paper would benefit from a clearer and more complete specification of the verifier training procedure, additional statistical reporting, and stronger ablations to isolate the specific contribution of backward verification beyond “more proposer updates.” Some metric choices (agreement with EM) and partial reporting of LLM-judge scores also temper the strength of claims around logical verifiability. Overall, I view this as a solid, incremental step with a useful perspective on proposer optimization in self-play. With clarifications and additional analyses as outlined, it would be a worthwhile contribution for NeurIPS.
